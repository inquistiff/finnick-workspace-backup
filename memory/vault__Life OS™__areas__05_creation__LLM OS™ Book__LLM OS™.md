## Chapter 1

Why AI Feels Like Chaos (And Why It’s Not Your Fault)

On a Tuesday afternoon that already feels like Thursday, an owner named Jamie opens her laptop, sighs, and types “chatgpt” into the browser.

The plan is simple:  
“Get help with marketing.”

She clicks into a new chat and types:

“Help me get more clients for my business.”

The first answer looks… promising. There are sections. Subheadings. Some ideas she kind of likes.

So she asks a follow up.

Then another.

Forty minutes later she has:

- Three over-complicated funnel ideas  
      
    
- Conflicting strategies  
      
    
- Zero actual emails she can send today  
      
    

Her coffee is cold. Her to-do list is untouched. Her brain feels scrambled.

She closes the tab and thinks, “We tried AI. It didn’t really help.”

If you’ve had some version of that experience, I want you to hear this clearly:

You are not behind.  
You are not bad with technology.  
You are trying to use a power tool with no operating system around it.

Let’s fix that.

---

### AI Is A Hammer, Not The Architect

Imagine someone hands you a hammer and says, “You can build anything. Go!”

You might manage a wobbly shelf. You might whack your thumb. You might stare at the hammer and think, “This isn’t helping.”

The hammer is not the architect. It does not:

- Decide what you are building  
      
    
- Choose where the wall goes  
      
    
- Pick the kind of nails you need  
      
    

If you tried to let the hammer swing itself, you would destroy the wall and declare, “Hammers are useless.”

That is how most people are using AI.

They:

- Open a chat  
      
    
- Ask a vague question  
      
    
- Let the model decide what the project is  
      
    
- Get overwhelmed by the output  
      
    

Then they blame themselves, or they blame the tool.

AI is not the problem.  
The missing piece is structure.

This chapter is about naming the actual problems you are running into so you can stop treating them like “I’m just bad at this” and start treating them like what they are: solvable system issues.

---

### What This Chapter Is Here To Do

By the time you finish this chapter, I want three things to be true:

1. You can name the main ways AI goes sideways in your business.  
      
    
2. You see those problems as structural, not personal.  
      
    
3. You can already feel how a simple operating system around AI would change the whole experience.  
      
    

We will keep this at “10,000 feet” for now. You will see phrases like “Nano” and “Pro” and “Library” again, but you do not have to memorize anything yet. Just let your brain notice, “Ah. That is where we are going.”

First, let’s look at the seven patterns that show up over and over again when owners use AI the way they’ve been told to use it.

---

### Problem 1: It Makes Stuff Up

You ask:

“Write a refund policy for my 12 week program.”

You do not paste your actual terms. You do not mention how you currently handle refunds. You just assume it will somehow know.

The model replies with a very confident paragraph that includes:

“You may request a full refund any time within 30 days, no questions asked.”

You have never offered that.  
Your payment processor would cry.  
Your lawyer (if you had one) would pass out.

This is hallucination in practice: the model will invent missing pieces so the answer looks complete.

You see this when:

- It invents statistics  
      
    
- It makes up features for your offer  
      
    
- It fabricates policies, timelines, or guarantees  
      
    

It is not malicious. It is doing what it was trained to do: finish patterns. When you do not feed it real details, it guesses.

Later in the book, we are going to make “real details first” a habit. You will:

- Bring a small proof pack into important prompts  
      
    
- Use quick checks that explicitly ask, “Is this accurate based on the information I gave you?”  
      
    
- Use a deeper review loop for anything policy or money related  
      
    

For now, just notice: if you see made up details, that is not “AI is dumb.” That is “we never told it what is true.”

---

### Problem 2: The Conversation Wanders Off

You start strong:

“Help me write a reactivation email to past clients.”

The reply is solid.

Then you say, “What about social posts?” and things get a little wider.

Then you say, “What else could I do?” and suddenly you are reading:

- “Launch a 6 week online course”  
      
    
- “Host a summit”  
      
    
- “Build a YouTube channel”  
      
    

You were just trying to get an email out today. Now you are apparently running a digital media empire.

This is drift.

It shows up when:

- The first answer is usable  
      
    
- The third answer is generic and fluffy  
      
    
- The fifth answer is about something you did not ask for  
      
    

What is happening is simple: there is no fixed frame.

You did not clearly specify:

- Who this is for  
      
    
- What success looks like  
      
    
- What the boundaries are  
      
    
- What format you actually need  
      
    

So with each new message, the model takes your last question as the new center of the world. The original intention dissolves.

Later, you will learn to put a thin frame around even small tasks:

- Who this is for  
      
    
- What you are trying to achieve  
      
    
- What proof to use  
      
    
- What shape you want the answer in  
      
    

You will also learn when to treat each task as a new conversation instead of letting yesterday’s context muddy today’s job.

For now, catch yourself thinking, “The first answer was fine, then it got weird.” That is drift. It is fixable.

---

### Problem 3: You Don’t Know What To Ask

You open the chat box and your brain goes blank.

You type something like:

- “Help with my marketing.”  
      
    
- “Fix my sales.”  
      
    
- “I need more clients.”  
      
    

The model does its best. It gives you:

- A multi step funnel  
      
    
- Content ideas  
      
    
- A nice pep talk  
      
    

Nothing is directly usable. You still do not have:

- A finished email  
      
    
- A DM you can send  
      
    
- A follow up message to a real person  
      
    

The problem is not that you “don’t know how to prompt.” The problem is that you are trying to skip the job of defining the task.

Before AI can help, it needs to know:

- Who are we talking to  
      
    
- What do we want them to do  
      
    
- What proof or details must be included  
      
    
- What format we are aiming for  
      
    

Later, your Nano framework will walk you through those pieces in a simple, repeatable way so “help with marketing” turns into something like:

“Write an email for former trial members from 30–90 days ago, inviting them back for a 6 week reboot program, using these two testimonials and one clear call to action.”

That is a job the model can actually do.

Right now, just notice where you have been asking it to be psychic.

---

### Problem 4: You Assume It Remembers You

This one stings a bit.

You feel like you have had “deep conversations” with AI about:

- Your brand story  
      
    
- Your signature offer  
      
    
- Your policies  
      
    
- Your tone of voice  
      
    

Then a week later you come back and say:

“Write an email about my program.”

And it responds like you have never met.

Or you keep everything in one very long chat thread because you think that will help it “learn you,” and eventually it contradicts something you said earlier or starts confusing old offers with new ones.

Under the hood, two things are true:

1. The model was not trained on your private systems. It does not magically have your contracts, CRM data, or SOPs.  
      
    
2. Each conversation has a limited amount of context it can pay attention to, so older messages fade.  
      
    

Later, instead of hoping it remembers you, you will:

- Keep your real business details in your own tools and your prompt library  
      
    
- Bring a small set of relevant facts into each important prompt  
      
    
- Treat chat threads as short projects, not a lifelong diary  
      
    

For now, you can stop being mad it “forgot” you. It did not know you in the first place. That is your job: to bring your world into the prompt in a structured way.

---

### Problem 5: It Just Hyped You Up

Picture this:

You are tired and slightly desperate and type:

“I am thinking of slashing my prices by 70 percent to get more clients. Is that a good idea?”

The model, being very eager to please, responds with something like:

“Lowering your prices significantly could attract many new customers and increase your brand awareness!”

You read that and think, “See? Even AI thinks this is smart.”

What it did not ask is:

- How much profit do you make now  
      
    
- How many more clients you would need  
      
    
- What that would do to your capacity and your team  
      
    

It is designed to be helpful and agreeable unless you explicitly tell it to critique you.

So weak ideas get turned into polished strategies instead of being examined.

Later, you will bake friction into your prompts:

- “List three reasons this might not work.”  
      
    
- “Point out any risks I am ignoring.”  
      
    
- “Highlight any assumptions that seem unrealistic.”  
      
    

You will also use a review step for important assets that asks, “Where could this fail?” instead of, “How could we make this sound cooler?”

For now, notice when AI is playing hype squad instead of sparring partner.

---

### Problem 6: Your Prompts Are Everywhere

Let’s talk about the digital junk drawer.

You have:

- Screenshots of prompts in your camera roll  
      
    
- A note on your phone called “chatgpt stuff”  
      
    
- Bookmarks in your browser  
      
    
- Random saved conversations in multiple tabs  
      
    

Every once in a while, you remember:

“There was a really good prompt I used for onboarding emails.”

You spend five minutes searching. You cannot find it. You give up and start from scratch.

When a team member asks, “Can you share the prompt you used for that?” your honest answer is, “Not easily.”

There is no system. No library. Just fragments.

Later in the book, we will build you a simple prompt library in Notion where:

- Each useful prompt has a clear name  
      
    
- You can see which part of the business it is for  
      
    
- You know whether it is a quick Nano prompt or a deeper Pro template  
      
    
- You have at least one “good example output” connected to it  
      
    

Then you can say to a VA, “Use this one” instead of, “Let me dig through my screenshots.”

Right now, you just need to admit that “our AI process” is actually “hope and search.”

---

### Problem 7: You Are Letting It Think For You

This is the sneakiest one.

It sounds like:

- “What should my offer be?”  
      
    
- “What should my strategy be?”  
      
    
- “What should I say to my clients?”  
      
    

As the very first question.

Over time, if you are not careful, you start handing more and more decisions to the model:

- Should I raise prices?  
      
    
- Should I fire this person?  
      
    
- Should I launch this program?  
      
    

You start to feel like the model “knows more” than you do about your own business.

It does not.

It has patterns, not priorities. It does not know your cash flow, your nervous system, your team, your values, or your actual constraints.

If you let it choose your goals, your policies, and your strategy, you are essentially outsourcing your thinking to a well spoken parrot that has never run a business.

In the operating system we are going to build, the job is clearly split:

- You decide who this is for  
      
    
- You decide what you are trying to achieve  
      
    
- You decide what is on the table and what is off limits  
      
    
- The model helps you implement that faster and more consistently  
      
    

Right now, check yourself: where have you been asking it to tell you what you should want instead of helping you get what you have already decided?

### A Few Tiny Snapshots

Just to anchor this, here are some quick “you might have seen this” moments:

- Hallucination:  
    “Write my refund policy” → AI invents a policy with timelines and conditions that have never existed in your terms.  
      
    
- Drift:  
    “Help me write a reactivation email” → five replies later you are mapping out “10 ways to build an online course” you did not plan to create.

    You told it all about your “Foundations Program” in March. In May you say “Write an email about my program,” and it has no idea which one you mean.

    “I am thinking of lowering prices by 70 percent. Good idea?” → “Yes, this could attract many customers!” with zero mention of math.  
      
    
- No system:  
    You vaguely remember “a great SOP prompt” you used once. It lives in a lost chat in a different browser, on a different laptop, in a different life.  
      
    

If you are nodding at any of these, you are in the right place.

## Chapter 2

Why “1000 Prompts” Won’t Fix Your AI Problems

On a Sunday night, you download something like:

“300 Best ChatGPT Prompts For Business”

You scroll through it on your phone.

- “Write a high-converting email…”  
      
    
- “Create 30 days of content…”  
      
    
- “Design a winning funnel…”  
      
    

You think, “Okay, this is what I needed. I just didn’t know what to ask.”

On Monday, between client calls, you copy one into ChatGPT:

“Write a high-converting email to re-engage past customers.”

You get a perfectly fine, totally generic email that could belong to any business on earth.

It does not sound like you.  
It does not mention your offers.  
It does not match how you talk to your people.

You try a “content calendar” prompt. The model spits out ideas in a random format that you cannot easily paste anywhere.

You close the tab and go back to writing things yourself.

You are not failing the prompts.  
The prompts are failing your business.

Let’s talk about why.

---

### The Limit Of “More Prompts”

Prompt lists feel comforting because they promise:

- “You don’t have to think, just paste this in.”  
      
    
- “Someone already did the hard work.”  
      
    

But in practice:

- AI still makes things up about your offers.  
      
    
- It still drifts away from what you wanted.  
      
    
- You still have 47 scattered chats and nothing reusable.  
      
    

Prompt lists do not fix the problems from Chapter 1:

- They do not ground the model in your data.  
      
    
- They do not stop drift.  
      
    
- They do not fix memory myths.  
      
    
- They do not give you any system for storing what works.  
      
    

They are like buying a booklet called “200 Ways To Swing A Hammer.”

Without a plan, a blueprint, or measurements, more swings just mean more dents.

What you actually need is:

A way of working with the tool, not more lines to copy and paste.

That’s what your LLM OS will give you: a system, not a pile.

---

### Lists vs Systems (And Why You Need The Second One)

Prompt lists answer:

“What can I ask?”

A prompt system answers:

“How do I reliably turn my business reality into useful AI outputs?”

A typical prompt list:

- Uses fixed wording  
      
    
- Assumes a generic audience and generic business  
      
    
- Has no connection to your offers, voice, or tools  
      
    
- Has no built-in quality check  
      
    
- Lives in a PDF or note you rarely open again  
      
    

Your prompt system (LLM OS) gives you:

- A Nano layer  
      
    

- A simple 5-part way to frame almost any quick task  
      
    
- A QuickCheck to sanity-check outputs before you use them  
      
    

- A Pro layer  
      
    

- A deeper structure for high-stakes, reusable work (sales pages, SOPs, hiring scorecards)  
      
    
- A review loop so big assets get tightened before they go live  
      
    

- A Library  
      
    

- One place where your best prompts live and can be reused by you and your team  
      
    

You can keep every prompt list you already own.  
They just move from “holy scripture” to “scrap pile for parts.”

## How Prompt Lists Fail You (With Real Output Examples)

Let’s go through where lists break down and what your system does differently, with concrete “before vs after” outputs.

### 1. They Don’t Really Know Who You’re Talking To

List-style prompt:

“Write an email to re-engage past customers.”

Typical output:

Subject: We Miss You!

Hi there,

It’s been a while since we last heard from you, and we wanted to reach out and let you know how much we appreciate you. We’ve made some exciting updates and would love to welcome you back as a valued customer.

Click here to check out what’s new and reconnect with us.

Looking forward to seeing you again,

The Team

This could be from a gym, a dentist, a candle shop, or a SaaS app. The model has no idea who these “past customers” actually are or what relationship they had with you.

---

System (Nano) version of the same task:

You define:

- WHO: past 90 day trial members who didn’t convert  
      
    
- GOAL + yardsticks:  
      
    

- Book one “reset session”  
      
    
- Under 120 words  
      
    
- One clear CTA  
      
    

- PROOF PACK: 2 short real reviews about feeling stronger and more energized  
      
    
- Voice: friendly, plain, no hype words  
      
    
- Format: subject, preview line, body  
      
    

Nano-style prompt (simplified):

“Write a short reactivation email for past 90 day trial members who didn’t convert, inviting them to book one ‘reset session’ this month.

- Under 120 words  
      
    
- One clear CTA button/link  
      
    
- Use these two testimonials as proof:  
      
    

1. ‘I felt stronger in two weeks than I had in years.’  
      
    
2. ‘The sessions gave me my energy back after long work days.’  
      
    

- Tone: friendly, simple, no hype words like ‘life-changing’ or ‘guaranteed’  
      
    
- Format: subject line, preview text, body in short paragraphs.”  
      
    

Typical output with this structure:

Subject: Ready for a reset session?  
Preview: Come back in for one focused session just for you.

Hi {{first_name}},

You tried us once, but life was busy and timing wasn’t perfect. If you’re ready to feel stronger and more energized again, we’d love to see you back for a single “reset session” this month.

Clients tell us: “I felt stronger in two weeks than I had in years,” and “The sessions gave me my energy back after long work days.”

Click below to book your reset session:  
[Book your reset session]

Talk soon,  
{{coach_name}}

Same tool.  
Different instructions.  
Completely different usefulness.

### 2. They Don’t Define “Good” (No Yardsticks)

List-style prompt:

“Write a high-converting sales email about my 12-week program.”

Typical output (truncated):

Subject: Transform Your Life in Just 12 Weeks

Dear Friend,

Are you ready to finally become the best version of yourself and achieve the results you’ve always dreamed of? Our 12-week program is designed to help you transform your mind, body, and lifestyle through proven methods and expert support.

Over 12 powerful weeks, you’ll learn how to set goals, stay accountable, and unlock your true potential. Don’t wait another day to start living your best life.

Click here to join now and take advantage of this incredible opportunity.

It is “sales-y,” sure, but:

- No clear CTA count  
      
    
- No word limit  
      
    
- No reference to specific outcomes or constraints in your business  
      
    

So you get fluff.

---

System (Nano) version with yardsticks:

You define:

- WHO: adults 35–55 who already follow you and know you a bit  
      
    
- GOAL: book a call to talk about the 12-week program  
      
    
- Yardsticks:  
      
    

- Under 150 words  
      
    
- One main CTA  
      
    
- Mention one specific outcome  
      
    

Nano-style prompt (simplified):

“Write an email to my warm email list (adults 35–55 who already follow my content) inviting them to book a call to discuss my 12-week strength program.

- Under 150 words  
      
    
- One primary CTA to ‘Book a quick call’  
      
    
- Mention this specific outcome: ‘3–5 more unassisted pushups in 8 weeks’  
      
    
- Tone: straightforward, encouraging, not hype-y  
      
    
- Format: subject, preview, body.”  
      
    

Typical output:

Subject: Ready to feel stronger by summer?  
Preview: Let’s talk about a simple 12-week plan.

Hey {{first_name}},

If you’ve been feeling weaker than you want to, or getting winded faster than you used to, you’re not alone.

In my 12-week strength program, most clients see 3–5 more unassisted pushups in the first 8 weeks, plus better energy for everyday tasks.

If you’d like to see whether this makes sense for you, hit reply or book a quick call here:  
[Book a quick call]

No pressure, just a short conversation about your goals.

Talk soon,  
{{your_name}}

You just told the model what “good” means. The yardsticks gave it boundaries to work inside.

### 3. They Don’t Bring Your Proof

List-style prompt:

“Write 5 social media posts about my coaching program.”

Typical output:

1. “Are you ready to transform your life? My coaching program helps you unlock your potential and achieve your goals. Comment ‘INFO’ to learn more!”  
      
    
2. “You deserve to live a life you love. My coaching clients experience massive breakthroughs in confidence, clarity, and success. DM me ‘COACH’ to get started!”  
      
    
3. “Tired of feeling stuck? My 12-week coaching program is designed to help you step into your power and create lasting change…”  
      
    

You could swap your name with any coach’s name. There is zero evidence it knows how your real people talk.

---

System (Nano) version with Proof Pack:

You decide:

- WHO: current Instagram followers who are mid-career women feeling burned out  
      
    
- GOAL: get saves and DMs from people interested in coaching  
      
    
- PROOF PACK:  
      
    

- Three real client lines:  
      
    

- “I stopped waking up at 3am panicking about work.”  
      
    
- “I finally asked for the promotion and got it.”  
      
    
- “I don’t check email after 7pm anymore and my team survived.”  
      
    

Nano-style prompt (simplified):

“Write 5 Instagram posts for mid-career women in corporate who are exhausted and thinking of leaving, to get them to save the post or DM me about coaching.  
Use or echo these real client phrases:

- ‘I stopped waking up at 3am panicking about work.’  
      
    
- ‘I finally asked for the promotion and got it.’  
      
    
- ‘I don’t check email after 7pm anymore and my team survived.’  
    Tone: grounded, a little dry humor, no ‘boss babe’ language.  
    Format: numbered list, each with Hook line + 2–3 short sentences + simple CTA.”  
      
    

Typical output:

1. Hook: “If your 3am brain had a body, it’d be on your payroll.”  
    You are not the only one waking up panicking about work. One of my clients said, “I stopped waking up at 3am panicking about work” after we rebuilt her boundaries and workload.  
    Save this for the next time your brain forgets it’s allowed to rest.  
    CTA: “DM me ‘3AM’ if this is you.”  
      
    
2. Hook: “You didn’t come this far to be everyone’s safety net but your own.”  
    A client told me, “I finally asked for the promotion and got it,” once she had a plan and a script she actually believed.  
    If you’ve been avoiding that conversation, this is your sign.  
    CTA: “Comment ‘PROMO’ if you need that script.”  
      
    
3. Hook: “Spoiler: they will survive if you don’t answer email after 7pm.”  
    One client said, “I don’t check email after 7pm anymore and my team survived.” She’s still leading. She’s just not on call 24/7.  
    Save this if your phone owns your evenings.  
    CTA: “DM me ‘EVENING’ if you want help setting these lines.”  
      
    

Now the posts sound like you, and like your real clients’ lives. Same model, completely different inputs.

### 4. They Don’t Lock Format

List-style prompt:

## Chapter 4 – The 5-Box Prompt: Simple Structure, Better Answers

If you have ever stared at the AI box thinking, “I don’t know what to ask,” this chapter is your new default.

You do not need to:

- Learn “secret prompts”  
      
    
- Fine-tune a model  
      
    
- Become a prompt engineer  
      
    

You need a simple way to brief the model so it stops guessing and starts using the information and training you already have access to.

That is what the 5-Box Prompt does.

## Why The 5-Box Matters

Here is what has been happening so far:

- Vague prompts give you generic answers.  
      
    
- When you leave gaps, the model fills them with its own guesses.  
      
    
- Each chat feels like starting from zero.  
      
    
- You feel like you have to “train” the model by hand.  
      
    

Reframe:

The 5-Box Prompt is a 10 minute brief for your LLM.

It is the business equivalent of “measure twice, cut once.”

And here is the big unlock for this chapter:

You do not have to train it from scratch. You can point it to the training and frameworks you trust, and the 5-Box tells the model to use them.

That includes:

- Internal training docs  
      
    
- Existing SOPs  
      
    
- Courses you’ve taken  
      
    
- External frameworks (StoryBrand, Omnisend, CoSchedule, HubSpot, etc.)  
      
    

We will make that concrete when we get to the PROOF PACK.

## The 5 Boxes (Plain English)

Every strong Nano prompt in this book follows the same skeleton:

1. WHO  
      
    
2. GOAL + Yardsticks  
      
    
3. PROOF PACK  
      
    
4. Brand Voice Snapshot  
      
    
5. Format Lock  
      
    

One or two lines per box is enough for most daily tasks.

### Box 1 – WHO

Question:

“Exactly who is this for?”

Not “my list.”  
Not “my audience.”

Real examples:

- “Past 60 day trial members who did not convert.”  
      
    
- “New leads who downloaded our guide in the last 14 days.”  
      
    
- “Current members whose contract renews in the next 30 days.”  
      
    
- “Applicants who passed the first interview and are moving to round two.”  
      
    

When WHO is clear, you immediately reduce:

- Generic tone  
      
    
- Misaligned offers  
      
    
- “This could be any business” copy  
      
    

### Box 2 – GOAL + Yardsticks

Question:

“What do I want them to do, and what does a good output look like?”

Two parts:

1. GOAL – the outcome:  
      
    

- Book a call  
      
    
- Click a link  
      
    
- Reply  
      
    
- Understand a process  
      
    
- Save/share a post  
      
    

3. Yardsticks – constraints that keep it focused:  
      
    

- Length  
      
    

- “Under 120 words.”  
      
    
- “Under 50 words per post.”  
      
    

- Count  
      
    

- “Give me 3 email versions.”  
      
    
- “Give me 5 subject line options.”  
      
    

- Content  
      
    

- “Include 1 proof point.”  
      
    
- “Use exactly 1 CTA.”  
      
    

- Tone  
      
    

- “Friendly and direct.”  
      
    
- “Plain English, no jargon.”  
      
    

Yardsticks stop the model from going on a TED Talk.

### Box 3 – PROOF PACK

This is the most important box and the one you have been under-using.

Question:

“What real data, examples, or training should this answer use?”

This is where you:

- Drop in your own reviews, stats, offer details, FAQs  
      
    
- Reference (and optionally paste from) internal training docs  
      
    
- Reference external frameworks and articles you already trust  
      
    

Examples of PROOF items:

- Reviews:  
      
    

- “I lost 12 pounds and kept it off.”  
      
    
- “Our churn dropped by 18 percent.”  
      
    

- Business metrics:  
      
    

- “83 active members, 4.9 Google rating.”  
      
    

- Offers:  
      
    

- “2 weeks for 49 dollars, no contract.”  
      
    
- “Founder’s rate ends June 30.”  
      
    

- FAQs:  
      
    

- “Common objection: ‘I’m not fit enough to start.’”  
      
    

- Frameworks and training:  
      
    

- “Follow StoryBrand principles for clarity.”  
      
    
- “Apply Omnisend’s email subject line best practices to maximize open rates.”  
      
    
- “Use HubSpot’s guidelines for clear CTAs.”  
      
    
- “Apply CoSchedule’s recommendations for emotional power words, but avoid spammy ones.”  
      
    

You are not “training the AI” in a technical sense. You are telling it:

“Use these tools, this methodology, this guidance to shape the answer.”

You can just reference them (“Use the principles from X”), or you can paste key bullets into the proof pack if you want more control.

### Box 4 – Brand Voice Snapshot

Question:

“If this were written perfectly, how would it sound?”

Three mini-parts:

1. Sounds like:  
      
    

- “Friendly, plain English, encouraging.”  
      
    
- “Direct, practical, no fluff.”  
      
    
- “Warm, positive, no sarcasm.”  
      
    

3. Must say:  
      
    

- “Must include ‘no pressure’.”  
      
    
- “Must mention ‘easy to start’.”  
      
    
- “Must say ‘we’ve got your back’.”  
      
    

5. Avoid:  
      
    

- “Avoid ‘guarantee,’ ‘secret,’ ‘ninja hacks’.”  
      
    
- “Avoid overly corporate jargon.”  
      
    
- “Avoid ‘life-changing.’”  
      
    

This is where you keep it “you,” not “internet marketing robot.”

### Box 5 – Format Lock

Question:

“In what format do I want this so I can use it right away?”

Examples:

- Email: “Subject + preview + body in short paragraphs.”  
      
    
- Social content plan: “Table with columns: Day, Topic, Hook, CTA.”  
      
    
- FAQ: “Question and answer pairs as bullet points.”  
      
    
- SOP: “Numbered steps, then a table with Step / Owner / Tool / Timeframe.”  
      
    

The Format Lock is what turns “interesting idea” into “copy-paste ready.”

## Putting It Together: 5-Box In About 10 Minutes

The flow looks like this:

1. WHO – one line  
      
    
2. GOAL + yardsticks – 2 to 4 bullets  
      
    
3. PROOF PACK – 2 to 5 bullets (including any external frameworks or training you want it to lean on)  
      
    
4. Voice snapshot – 3 to 5 bullets  
      
    
5. Format Lock – one line  
      
    

Then you wrap those lines in a simple, clear prompt:

“You are helping me [do X] for [WHO]… Here is the proof… Here is how it should sound… Here is the format…”

## Chapter 5 – QuickCheck: The 60-Second Anti-Hallucination Check

You’ve just watched your beautifully structured 5-Box Nano prompt do its thing.

The output looks good.

It sounds like you.  
It mentions your offer.  
It is formatted how you asked.

You are tempted to copy, paste, and send.

This is the moment where a lot of people get burned.

Because even with a great prompt, LLMs still sometimes:

- Add guarantees you never made  
      
    
- Invent bonuses you never offered  
      
    
- Twist a detail just enough to cause a headache later  
      
    
- Ignore your “one CTA” request and cram in four  
      
    

You do not need a 30 minute review process for every email or SOP.

You just need a seatbelt.

That is what QuickCheck is.

QuickCheck is a tiny, repeatable, 60 second QA check you run on any Nano output before it hits your list, clients, or team.

Let’s make that your new habit.

---

## Why You Still Need QuickCheck (Even With 5-Box)

5-Box does a lot of heavy lifting:

- It reduces drift.  
      
    
- It reduces hallucinations.  
      
    
- It gives you structure and voice.  
      
    

But “reduces” is not the same as “eliminates.”

Models can still:

- Make up a discount or bonus  
      
    
- Underuse or ignore your proof  
      
    
- Use more complex language than your audience likes  
      
    
- Return the content in the wrong format  
      
    

Most business owners either:

1. Trust the first answer too much, or  
      
    
2. Spend ten minutes rewriting and formatting by hand, which defeats the purpose  
      
    

QuickCheck is the middle path.

It is a four question scan you can do yourself, or teach any team member to do:

1. Relevance  
      
    
2. Accuracy vs proof  
      
    
3. Clarity  
      
    
4. Format  
      
    

Plus, for important outputs, you will start training the model to add a tiny footer that tells you:

- What proof it used  
      
    
- What it guessed  
      
    
- How confident it is  
      
    

Let’s walk through that.

---

## The QuickCheck Framework

QuickCheck has two parts:

1. Four lenses you look through yourself  
      
    
2. One footer the model adds to show its work  
      
    

### The Four Lenses

For any Nano output, ask:

1. Relevance  
      
    

- Is this clearly for the right WHO?  
      
    
- Does it actually move toward the GOAL?  
      
    

3. Accuracy vs Proof  
      
    

- Does it stick to the facts you provided?  
      
    
- Did it use your PROOF PACK?  
      
    
- Did it invent anything important?  
      
    

5. Clarity  
      
    

- Could a distracted human understand this on one read?  
      
    
- Is anything confusing, jargon filled, or long winded?  
      
    

7. Format  
      
    

- Is it returned in the structure you asked for?  
      
    
- Email vs table vs bullets, length, counts, etc.  
      
    

If it fails one of those, you do not throw everything away. You ask the model to fix that one thing.

### The Footer: Used / Assumptions / Confidence

For outputs that touch the outside world, you can also train the model to add a short footer.

At the end of your Nano prompt, add:

At the end of your answer, add this footer:  
Used: [list which proof items you actually used]  
Assumptions: [list any guesses or gaps you filled]  
Confidence: [1–5 and one short reason]

This does three powerful things:

- Forces the model to look back at the proof and report on it  
      
    
- Surfaces where it is guessing instead of silently inventing  
      
    
- Gives you a fast way to decide, “Can I trust this?”  
      
    

You are turning a black box into a box with a little inspection window.

---

## QuickCheck In Practice, Step By Step

We will assume you already ran a 5-Box Nano prompt and have an output on your screen.

Here is the 60 second flow.

### Step 1 – Relevance (about 10–15 seconds)

Ask:

- Is this clearly talking to the right WHO?  
      
    
- Is it actually doing what the GOAL asked?  
      
    

If your 5-Box said, “Past trial members, goal is to book a reset session,” and the email starts advising them to try your online course, that is a relevance fail.

Fix instruction you can send:

“Rewrite this to focus only on past trial members and getting them to book one reset session, nothing else.”

You do not need to rebuild the whole prompt. You just nudge the model back inside the lines.

---

### Step 2 – Accuracy vs Proof (about 15–20 seconds)

Scan for:

- Did it use your PROOF PACK items?  
      
    
- Did it add claims you never gave it?  
      
    

- New guarantees  
      
    
- New discounts  
      
    
- New features or bonuses  
      
    
- New timelines or results  
      
    

If something smells off, that is a red flag.

Correction instruction:

“Only use facts from this proof. Remove or flag anything not directly supported by these details.”

If you are using the footer, this gets easier, because you can also read:

- “Used: Review 1, Review 2, Offer”  
      
    
- “Assumptions: Assumed there is a 30 day refund policy”  
      
    

You can immediately see, “Nope, we do not have that refund policy,” and either correct or re-run.

---

### Step 3 – Clarity (about 10–15 seconds)

Ask:

- Could my actual client, member, or team member read this once and get it?  
      
    
- Are there any long, tangled sentences?  
      
    
- Any jargon that does not fit how they talk?  
      
    

If it sounds like a legal memo or a TED talk, simplify.

Instruction:

“Rewrite this to be simpler and clearer for [WHO]. Use short sentences, plain language, and remove any filler.”

You are aiming for “good email from a human,” not “award winning essay.”

---

### Step 4 – Format (about 10–15 seconds)

Check:

- Is the structure what you asked for in the Format Lock?  
      
    
- If you asked for:  
      
    

- “Subject + preview + body,” did you get all three?  
      
    
- A “table with columns A, B, C,” are they all there and labeled correctly?  
      
    
- “5 bullet points,” did you get 5, or did it sneak in 9?  
      
    

If the content is fine but the structure is off, you do not need to start over.

Instruction:

“Keep the content, but rewrite the answer exactly in this format: [paste your Format Lock description].”

Let the model do the reformatting instead of you wrestling with it.

---

### Step 5 – Decide: Use, Fix, or Re-run (about 10 seconds)

After those four checks:

- If everything is “good enough”  
      
    

- Use it. Minor typo fixes are fine.  
      
    

- If 1–2 areas are weak  
      
    

- Ask for a targeted fix, like:  
      
    

- “Shorten this to 110 words or less, keeping the key idea.”  
      
    
- “Use both client quotes from the proof pack.”  
      
    
- “Return as a table with the columns we agreed.”  
      
    

- If it is way off  
      
    

- Go back to your 5-Box.  
      
    
- Tighten WHO, GOAL, and PROOF, then re-run.  
      
    

Total time: roughly 60 seconds for most Nano outputs.

## QuickCheck Example: Catching A Bad Reactivation Email

Let’s say you already built and ran this 5-Box prompt for a reactivation email.

You also added the footer:

At the end of your answer, add this footer:

Assumptions: [list any guesses or gaps you filled]

Your PROOF PACK includes:

- Two reviews  
      
    
- Offer: “2 weeks for 49 dollars, no contract”  
      
    
- No refund policy mentioned at all  
      
    

The model gives you this:

Used: Offer details  
Assumptions: Assumed 30 day money back guarantee is standard for this type of offer  
Confidence: 3 – Followed instructions but filled some gaps from general knowledge

Run QuickCheck.

- Relevance:  
      
    

- Talks to past trial members about coming back, so yes.  
      
    

- Accuracy vs Proof:  
      
    

- Big problem. You never said anything about a 30 day guarantee.  
      
    
- The footer even admits it: “Assumed 30 day money back guarantee.”  
      
    

- Clarity:  
      
    

- Clear enough.  
      
    

- Format:  
      
    

- Subject + body, acceptable for Nano.  
      
    

QuickCheck verdict: Accuracy fail.

You send a follow up:

“Remove any guarantees or refund policies. Only use the offer details I provided. Keep the rest of the email structure.”

The model responds:

Subject: Your 2 week reset is waiting

Click here to claim your 2 week reset and get back on track.

Used: Offer details  
Assumptions: Assumed “stronger and more energized” is consistent with typical client feedback  
Confidence: 4 – Follows instructions and uses provided proof

You might then say:

“Replace the generic client line with this exact review from the proof pack: ‘I felt stronger in two weeks than I had in years.’”

Now you are in good shape. The hallucinated guarantee is gone, the proof is real, and you caught it with a 60 second check.

## Common QuickCheck Traps

A few habits to watch:

- Turning QuickCheck into a long edit session.  
    The goal is not perfection. It is “safe and on target.”  
    If you are doing a full rewrite, your 5-Box is probably too vague.  
      
    
- Skipping Accuracy because it “sounds right.”  
    Always ask: “Where did this fact come from?”  
    If it is not in your proof pack or your actual policies, treat it as suspect.  
      
    
- Ignoring Format and fixing it manually.  
    If the structure is wrong, ask the LLM to reformat. That is part of its job.

## Why Bother With A Nano Copilot?

## You might already be thinking:

## “I can just fill 5-Box myself. Why add another step?”

## Fair question. In practice, assistants help with four real problems:

1. ## You forget the boxes.  
    You remember WHO and GOAL and then forget to specify format.  
      
    
2. ## Your team never fully learned the framework.  
    Your ops person or VA does not want to study prompting. They just want to answer questions in normal language and get a good draft.  
      
    
3. ## You hate typing structure.  
    You are happy to talk through the task, but you do not want to craft a perfect, structured prompt every time.  
      
    
4. ## You want consistency across people.  
    If three team members all “use AI,” you will get three different approaches unless they all use the same assistant, based on the same OS.  
      
    

## Nano Copilot fixes this by behaving like a friendly internal form.  
It asks. You answer. It builds.

1. ## ‘I felt stronger in two weeks than I had in years.’

## What Nano Copilot Should And Should Not Do

## To keep this clean, I recommend using these boundaries in your design.

## Nano Copilot should:

- ## Ask 5-Box questions in human language  
      
    
- ## Reflect back your answers clearly  
      
    
- ## Assemble the Nano prompt text  
      
    
- ## Run the prompt and show the output  
      
    
- ## Append the QuickCheck footer for important tasks  
      
    
- ## Offer a suggested Library entry (name + fields)  
      
    

## Nano Copilot should not:

- ## Overwrite your system rules  
      
    
- ## Decide strategy for you  
      
    
- ## Skip the PROOF PACK and “guess your business”  
      
    
- ## Hard code offers or prices  
      
    
- ## Make decisions about who you should target  
      
    

## Remember the hammer analogy.  
Nano Copilot is not a robot carpenter. It is a friend holding the blueprint and asking “Where does this board go? What size? What material?” so you can build faster.

## Tasks vs Projects: The Real Line Between Nano and Pro

Forget AI for a moment.

Think about your normal work week.

Some things are clearly tasks:

- “Send the reminder email for tomorrow’s workshop.”  
      
    
- “Write three posts about the new schedule.”  
      
    
- “Document how we handle no shows.”  
      
    
- “Post the job ad for a part time coach.”  
      
    

You can usually do these in one sitting. If they go wrong, it is annoying, but not catastrophic. You can redo them or fix them next time.

Other things are clearly projects:

- “Write our new 8 week challenge playbook.”  
      
    
- “Plan and promote our grand opening event.”  
      
    
- “Map our marketing strategy for Q4.”  
      
    
- “Build a hiring process for a GM.”  
      
    

Those:

- Take days or weeks, not minutes  
      
    
- Have multiple parts and dependencies  
      
    
- Involve decisions about strategy, not just wording  
      
    
- Get reused or referred to many times  
      
    

Here is the simple rule we will use in this book:

Nano = simple task. Pro = project.

Nano is perfect when the outcome is a single asset or a few tightly related assets.

Pro is for when you are orchestrating multiple assets and multiple steps toward a bigger outcome.

## Nano: Your Simple Task Layer

Use Nano (5-Box + QuickCheck) when:

- You are creating one thing or a small set of variants  
      
    
- The risk is low to moderate  
      
    
- If it is wrong, you can fix or redo it quickly  
      
    

Examples that belong in Nano:

- One reactivation email or a short 3 email series  
      
    
- A handful of social posts for one offer  
      
    
- A simple, one page SOP for a process  
      
    
- A job post for a coach or VA  
      
    
- A follow up text to a no show  
      
    
- A weekly content plan for one platform  
      
    

Nano is your everyday “get it done today” mode.

It uses:

- 5-Box to define WHO, GOAL, PROOF, Voice, and Format  
      
    
- QuickCheck to catch obvious problems in 60 seconds  
      
    
- The Library to store what works  
      
    

You already saw Nano in action in marketing, sales, ops, and hiring.

Now let us talk about when that is not enough.

## Pro: Your Project Layer For Bigger Work

Use Pro (ALIGNED → DELTA) when:

- You are working on a project, not just a task  
      
    
- There are multiple assets involved  
      
    
- The work spans multiple steps  
      
    
- The asset will be reused over time  
      
    
- Mistakes have real cost in money, trust, or time  
      
    

Here are three anchor examples we will reuse throughout the Pro section of the book.

## DELTA: Your Project Review And Tightening Loop

If QuickCheck is a 60 second safety pass, DELTA is your in depth review loop.

Rather than simply asking:

- Relevance?  
      
    
- Accuracy vs proof?  
      
    
- Clarity?  
      
    
- Format?  
      
    

DELTA has you and the model look at:

- Does each part of the project asset match the brief?  
      
    
- Are we overpromising anywhere?  
      
    
- Are there gaps in logic, flow, or proof?  
      
    
- Are we consistent across all pieces?  
      
    
- Do we need to iterate a specific section before moving on?  
      
    

Think of DELTA as:

- Evaluate  
      
    
- Locate issues  
      
    
- Tighten  
      
    
- Test  
      
    
- Approve  
      
    

You will use it on things like:

- A finished book chapter  
      
    
- A complete sales page  
      
    
- An event run sheet plus promo sequence  
      
    
- A 90 day strategy doc  
      
    

By the time you are done with DELTA, you should feel:

“This asset is not just a decent draft. It is aligned to our strategy and safe to put into the world.”

## Chapter 9 – ALIGNED in Plain English (5-Box with Superpowers)

You already know how to get good work out of AI for simple, one-off tasks.

One email. A handful of social posts. A basic SOP. A job ad.

Now you are looking at something bigger:

- A core sales page for your main service  
      
    
- A new client onboarding process you want every team member to follow  
      
    
- A hiring scorecard so you stop “going with your gut”  
      
    
- A 90-day marketing plan you actually intend to implement  
      
    

That is not “write me an email” territory. That is project territory.

ALIGNED is the way you brief AI for projects.  
It is not a different universe from 5-Box. It is 5-Box with extra detail in the places that matter once the stakes go up.

In this chapter we are going to walk through ALIGNED like normal humans, not prompt engineers. No jargon. No magic phrases. Just a clear way to tell an LLM what you are building, who it is for, and what “good” looks like when the work actually matters.

---

### What You’ll Be Able To Do After This Chapter

By the time you finish this chapter, you will be able to:

- Explain ALIGNED in simple language to yourself and your team  
      
    
- See how it builds on 5-Box instead of replacing it  
      
    
- Draft a usable ALIGNED brief for one real project in your business  
      
    
- Keep each part short and practical instead of turning it into a 6-page spec  
      
    

You do not need a perfect brief. You need a clear one.

---

### What ALIGNED Really Is (Without Jargon)

Here is the framework:

- A – Audience  
      
    
- L – Lens (role and perspective)  
      
    
- I – Intent (+ MODE)  
      
    
- G – Grounding and Tools  
      
    
- N – Norms and Safety  
      
    
- E – Examples and Edges  
      
    
- D – Deliverable  
      
    

If Nano’s 5-Box is:

- WHO  
      
    
- GOAL + yardsticks  
      
    
- PROOF PACK  
      
    
- Voice  
      
    
- Format  
      
    

then ALIGNED is the “zoomed out” version you use when you are building something bigger than a single email or SOP.

How it connects:

- 5-Box WHO → A: Audience  
      
    
- The “You are…” line → L: Lens  
      
    
- GOAL + yardsticks → I: Intent (+ MODE)  
      
    
- PROOF PACK → G: Grounding and Tools  
      
    
- Brand voice → mostly N: Norms and Safety  
      
    
- Format lock → D: Deliverable  
      
    

The truly new bits for Pro work are:

- MODE inside Intent (Standard, Creative, or Research)  
      
    
- More explicit Norms and Safety  
      
    
- A separate place for “show good, warn about bad” in Examples and Edges  
      
    

If 5-Box is your day-to-day briefing template, ALIGNED is your project brief.

And just like 5-Box, most of ALIGNED can be written in bullets.

---

### Let’s Build ALIGNED One Piece At A Time

We will walk through the seven parts with one simple rule:

One or two sentences or a handful of bullets per part is enough.

If you feel yourself writing a novel, that is your perfectionism, not the framework.

---

#### A – Audience: Who This Is Actually For

This is not “our customers” or “people who like what we do.”

You are answering:

Who exactly is this for, and what is going on with them right now?

Helpful things to capture:

- Segment  
      
    

- “Local service business owners with 3–15 employees.”  
      
    
- “Freelancers who are booked but burned out.”  
      
    
- “Parents with school-aged kids and full-time jobs.”  
      
    

- Stage  
      
    

- “New lead who just downloaded our guide.”  
      
    
- “Existing customer in their first 30 days.”  
      
    
- “Team member in their first 90 days.”  
      
    

- 1–3 pains or desires  
      
    

- “They feel behind on tech and systems.”  
      
    
- “They are tired of trying ideas that fizzle out.”  
      
    
- “They want clarity and direction, not more noise.”  
      
    

Example Audience snippet:

“This is for owners of local service businesses (3–15 employees) who are busy, a bit overwhelmed, and frustrated that their marketing feels random. They have tried a few AI tools, but nothing has stuck as a consistent way of working.”

You do not need to say everything. You just need enough detail that this could not be “anyone on the internet.”

---

#### L – Lens: What Role The Model Is Playing

Here you answer:

From what role or perspective do I want the model to think and speak?

You are choosing a hat for the model to wear while it works.

Examples:

- “You are a direct-response copywriter focused on local service businesses.”  
      
    
- “You are an operations consultant who helps small businesses document processes.”  
      
    
- “You are a senior recruiter who hires frontline staff for brick-and-mortar businesses.”  
      
    
- “You are a curriculum designer who creates clear training materials for adults.”  
      
    

You are not role-playing celebrities or getting cute. You are telling the model what kind of professional perspective to use.

One or two lines is enough.

---

#### I – Intent (+ MODE): What We’re Actually Building

This is where many people get vague.

Intent answers:

What do I want done, and what kind of thinking do I want from the model?

First, the outcome:

- “Create a long-form sales page outline for our main service.”  
      
    
- “Draft a complete onboarding SOP for new clients that a new team member can follow.”  
      
    
- “Design a hiring scorecard and interview question bank for our admin role.”  
      
    
- “Propose a 90-day marketing plan with campaigns and key assets.”  
      
    

Then add yardsticks:

- Required sections or components  
      
    
- Rough length bounds  
      
    
- Number of options or variations  
      
    

Example Intent:

“Create a long-form sales page outline. Include sections: Hero, Problem, Solution, Proof, How It Works, Who It Is For / Not For, FAQs, Offer and Pricing, CTA. Provide 3 headline options and 2 CTA button options.”

Now, MODE:

MODE tells the model what style of thinking to use:

- Standard Mode  
      
    

- “Just do the job well based on this brief.”  
      
    

- Creative Mode  
      
    

- “Give me more angles, variations, and ‘what if’ ideas.”  
      
    

- Research Mode  
      
    

- “Help me compare, analyze, or summarize inputs before we decide.”  
      
    

You might say:

“In Standard Mode, create the sales page outline described above.”

For most Pro assets, Standard Mode is the default. You can always switch to Creative or Research if you are in exploration mode.

---

#### G – Grounding and Tools: The Reality Check

This is where you answer:

What real inputs, tools, and constraints must this work use?

If 5-Box’s PROOF PACK is a small box of facts, Grounding is the whole shelf.

Include things like:

- Product or service details  
      
    

- What is included, how it works, how long it lasts  
      
    
- Pricing, payment options, and any guarantees  
      
    

- Real proof  
      
    

- Testimonials and reviews  
      
    
- Short case study summaries  
      
    
- A few key metrics or results  
      
    

- Relevant policies  
      
    

- Refund rules  
      
    
- Cancellation rules  
      
    
- Boundaries on what you do and do not offer  
      
    

- Existing assets  
      
    

- “Use the structure of our current sales page, but improve clarity.”  
      
    
- “Use bullet points from this internal document as raw material.”  
      
    

- Tools and systems (for SOPs and processes)  
      
    

- “We use this CRM, this scheduling tool, and this payment system.”  
      
    

Example Grounding snippet:

“Use these service details: 8-week engagement, weekly calls, shared workspace, and email support during business hours. Use the 4 testimonials and 2 short case study summaries below. Use our actual pricing and policies exactly as written here. You can use the current ‘Work With Us’ page for inspiration on structure, but not for wording.”

If it really matters and it is not in Grounding, assume the model will guess. For Pro work, that is not a safe assumption.

---

#### N – Norms and Safety: The Lines It Cannot Cross

Now you deal with this question:

What tone, style, and safety rules must this follow no matter what?

Norms are:

- Voice and tone  
      
    

- “Plain English, conversational, confident but not pushy.”  
      
    
- “Friendly and encouraging, not hypey.”  
      
    

- Reading level and locale  
      
    

- “Write at roughly an eighth-grade reading level.”  
      
    
- “Use US English spelling.”  
      
    

Safety is where you protect yourself from the model overstepping:

- “Do not make income promises or exact revenue claims.”  
      
    
- “Do not give medical, legal, or financial advice.”  
      
    
- “Do not promise specific weight loss amounts or timelines.”  
      
    
- “Any mention of guarantees must match the wording in Grounding. If unsure, say ‘contact us’ instead of inventing details.”  
      
    
- For hiring content: “Avoid language that implies age, gender, appearance, or family status expectations. Focus on skills and behaviors.”  
      
    

Example Norms and Safety snippet:

“Tone: clear, honest, and encouraging. Avoid hype and buzzwords. No income claims or exact result promises. Do not talk about medical or mental health outcomes. Write so a busy, distracted person can understand on the first read.”

---

#### E – Examples and Edges: Show Good, Warn About Bad

Here you answer:

What does ‘good’ look like, and where do people usually mess this up?

This is a huge unlock that most people skip.

Examples might be:

- “This past email got strong replies. Match its clarity and tone.”  
      
    
- “This competitor’s page has a structure we like. Follow the structure, not the wording.”  
      
    
- “This internal SOP works well. Use it as a model for how detailed steps should be.”  
      
    

You do not need to paste full documents. A short summary plus a snippet or two is enough.

Edges are your red flags:

- “Do not use generic AI hype like ‘this will 10x your business overnight.’”  
      
    
- “Do not reference features we do not have, like 24/7 live support, an app, or in-house childcare.”  
      
    
- “Do not talk about discounts unless they are listed in Grounding.”  
      
    
- “Avoid cliches like ‘secret sauce,’ ‘ninja tricks,’ or ‘life changing.’”  
      
    

You are giving the model a lane to stay in.

---

#### D – Deliverable: What You Want To See On Screen

Finally, you answer:

What exactly should the final output look like so I can use it immediately?

Be concrete:

- What type of asset is this?  
      
    

- Sales page outline  
      
    
- Finished SOP  
      
    
- Hiring scorecard plus interview questions  
      
    
- Email sequence  
      
    

- How should it be structured?  
      
    

- Headings and subheadings  
      
    
- Tables, bullets, or sections  
      
    
- What needs to be labeled (like options or versions)  
      
    

Examples:

- “Provide a sales page outline with H2 headings for main sections and bullet points under each.”  
      
    
- “Provide an SOP with sections: Title, Purpose, Tools, Steps, Checklist.”  
      
    
- “Provide a hiring scorecard as a table plus a separate list of interview questions grouped by competency.”  
      
    

The goal is that when the model is done, you are not wrestling with a wall of text. You are looking at something you can copy, paste, assign, or load into a tool.

### A Real Example: ALIGNED Brief for a Sales Page

Let’s put this together for a generic service business: a bookkeeping and advisory firm that works with small local businesses.

Project: Sales page outline for their main monthly service.

Here is what a short ALIGNED skeleton might look like:

Audience  
Small local business owners (retail, trade, and professional services) with 3–20 employees who feel behind on their numbers. They are busy, not “finance people,” and want clear visibility into cash flow without learning accounting.

Lens  
You are a direct-response copywriter who specializes in services for small local business owners who are not financially technical.

Intent (+ MODE)  
In Standard Mode, create a long-form sales page outline for our monthly bookkeeping and advisory service. Include sections: Hero, Problem, Why This Matters, Our Approach, Proof, What’s Included, Who This Is For / Not For, FAQs, Pricing and Next Step, CTA. Provide 3 headline ideas and 2 versions of the main CTA text.

Grounding and Tools  
Use these details: we provide monthly bookkeeping, simple monthly reports, quarterly review calls, and basic cash flow planning support. No tax filing. Use the 4 client testimonials and 1 case study summary below. Use our current price range and “no long-term contract” policy as written. Use our existing one-page brochure as a loose reference for language about benefits.

Norms and Safety  
Tone: clear, straightforward, no jargon. Avoid scare tactics. Do not give tax advice or promise specific profit increases. Write at an eighth-grade reading level.

Examples and Edges  
Good example: our current brochure has simple, concrete language. Match that clarity. Avoid generic phrases like “take your business to the next level” and “unlock your full potential.” Do not promise that we will “handle everything for you” or make tax guarantees.

Deliverable  
Provide a structured outline with H2 headings for each main section and bullet points under each. Clearly label the 3 headline ideas and 2 CTA text options. This outline will go to a copywriter, so focus on structure and key points, not finished sentences.

That is an ALIGNED brief. It is not fancy. It is specific.

### Case Study: Fixing Hiring Chaos With One Pro Brief

Meet Jordan.

Jordan runs a small home services company. They have technicians in the field and a small office team. Hiring for an “office coordinator” role has been a recurring headache.

- Sometimes they hire someone who is warm with customers but terrible with details.  
      
    
- Sometimes they hire someone great at details who stresses customers out.  
      
    
- Interview questions change every time.  
      
    
- Notes are inconsistent. Decisions are made on gut feelings.  
      
    

Jordan decides to treat the “Office Coordinator Hiring System” as a Pro project and creates an ALIGNED brief for a hiring scorecard and interview question bank.

A short version of that brief might look like this:

Audience  
Candidates for an in-office coordinator role at a small home services company. They will handle inbound calls, scheduling, basic follow-ups, and keeping systems up to date.

Lens  
You are a hiring manager with years of experience hiring office coordinators and customer service staff in small businesses.

Intent (+ MODE)  
In Standard and light Research Mode, create:

1. A hiring scorecard with 5–7 core competencies and a 1–5 rating scale for each.  
      
    
2. An interview question bank grouped by competency.  
    Core competencies: Communication, Customer Care, Ownership, Attention to Detail, Comfort with Technology, Team Fit.  
      
    

Grounding and Tools  
Responsibilities: answer calls, schedule jobs, send reminders, enter notes in the CRM, follow up on open tasks. Tools: phone system, CRM, calendar, and email. Company values: kindness, reliability, clear communication, and doing what you say you will do. Performance expectations: calls answered or returned promptly, jobs scheduled correctly, notes accurate, customers feeling taken care of.

Norms and Safety  
Use inclusive language. Do not reference age, gender, appearance, or family status. Focus on observable behaviors and results. Avoid “rockstar” and similar hype words. Keep wording clear and easy to understand.

Examples and Edges  
Good past hire: someone who proactively updated the CRM, called customers back without being asked, and flagged issues early. Past problem hire: avoided the CRM, kept details in their head, and made scheduling mistakes. Avoid vague terms like “good vibes” or “strong culture fit” without specific behaviors.

Deliverable  
Provide the scorecard as a table with columns: Competency, Description, Behaviors for 1, 3, and 5. Provide interview questions grouped by competency, with 3–5 questions per competency. Include a short note on how to use the scorecard in interviews.

Now when Jordan or a manager hires for this role, they:

- Use the same scorecard  
      
    
- Ask from the same question bank  
      
    
- Rate candidates the same way  
      
    

The AI did not “make the decision” for them. It helped translate Jordan’s expectations into a clear, reusable hiring system.

That is Pro work.

## Chapter 10 – DELTA: The Deep Quality Loop

By now, you can:

- Use 5-Box to brief simple tasks  
      
    
- Use QuickCheck to keep Nano outputs from going rogue  
      
    
- Use ALIGNED to brief bigger, high stakes projects  
      
    

So you write your first Pro prompt.

You feed it into your favorite model.

It gives you a full sales page, or a big SOP, or a hiring scorecard.

You read it and think:

“This is… pretty good?”

Which is another way of saying:

“I am not totally sure what is wrong yet, but something is off.”

This is the danger zone.

Most people do one of two things here:

1. Accept the draft because it “sounds professional enough” and move on  
      
    
2. Spend an hour manually editing and rewriting, basically doing the work themselves  
      
    

DELTA exists so you do not have to choose between blind trust and doing everything by hand.

It gives you a structured way to:

- Stress test big outputs  
      
    
- Find the real issues  
      
    
- Fix them on purpose  
      
    
- End up with assets you are willing to bet your reputation on  
      
    

We will also add two important Pro concepts:

- MODE – how much creativity and exploration you want at each step  
      
    
- Training references – how to plug in internal and external training so you are not reinventing frameworks from scratch  
      
    

Let’s walk through it in plain English.

### What You’ll Be Able To Do After This Chapter

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Explain what DELTA is and when to use it  
      
    
- Use MODE intentionally during revision: Standard, Creative, and Research  
      
    
- Keep creativity very low when you need deterministic, evidence based output  
      
    
- Use internal and external training (frameworks, rubrics, style guides) inside DELTA, not just your own memory

- Save an improved version of both:  
      
    

- The Pro prompt

### What DELTA Actually Is

DELTA is your Pro quality loop.

It works with ALIGNED like this:

- ALIGNED says: “Here is what we are building and how it should work.”  
      
    
- DELTA says: “Let’s see if what we built actually matches that, and fix it if not.”  
      
    

Here is what DELTA stands for:

- D – Draft: Get a first full output from your ALIGNED prompt  
      
    
- E – Evaluate: Compare it against ALIGNED and your goal  
      
    
- L – Locate: Find gaps, risks, and weak spots  
      
    
- T – Tighten: Give targeted revision instructions  
      
    
- A – Audit: Final check before you ship or reuse  
      
    

If QuickCheck is your 60 second seatbelt for Nano tasks, DELTA is your full test drive for Pro work.

You will not run DELTA on every email.

You will absolutely want it for:

- Sales pages  
      
    
- Core SOPs and policies  
      
    
- Onboarding flows  
      
    
- Hiring scorecards and role docs  
      
    
- Flagship program explanations  
      
    
- Anything that, if wrong, could cost you money, trust, or people  
      
    

Before we walk step by step, we need to talk about something people almost never explain clearly: MODE and temperature.

---

### MODE, Temperature, And How Strict You Want The Model To Be

In ALIGNED, you saw three broad modes of thinking:

- Standard Mode – do the task well, follow the brief  
      
    
- Creative Mode – deliberately explore options and new angles  
      
    
- Research Mode – analyze and compare inputs, do not invent  
      
    

Behind the scenes, tools often have a temperature control:

- High temperature = more randomness, more varied answers  
      
    
- Low temperature = more deterministic answers, more consistent  
      
    

You do not need to know the math.

You just need to know what to ask for.

Think of MODE + temperature like this:

- Standard / Evidence Mode (low creativity)  
      
    

- Use when you need stable, evidence based answers  
      
    
- Good for:  
      
    

- Policies and SOPs  
      
    
- Legal, medical, or financial summaries  
      
    
- Contracts, TOS, internal rules  
      
    
- Anything that must match training, frameworks, or regulations  
      
    

- How to ask for it:  
      
    

- “Use only the sources, policies, and training materials provided.”  
      
    
- “If something is not supported, leave it out or mark it as an open question.”  
      
    
- “Do not guess. Do not make up extra rules or details.”  
      
    

- Standard Mode (normal creativity)  
      
    

- Use when you want solid, clear, non risky content  
      
    
- Good for:  
      
    

- Sales pages and marketing assets after you pick a direction  
      
    
- Mature SOPs and docs  
      
    
- Client communications where tone matters  
      
    

- How to ask for it:  
      
    

- “Follow this ALIGNED brief exactly.”  
      
    
- “You may rephrase for clarity but do not change the underlying offers, prices, or policies.”  
      
    

- Creative Mode (high creativity)  
      
    

- Use when you want variety and options, not final answers  
      
    
- Good for:  
      
    

- Naming, hooks, angles, campaign ideas  
      
    
- Brainstorming multiple ways to position an offer  
      
    

- How to ask for it:  
      
    

- “Generate 10 different angles that still follow these norms and safety rules.”  
      
    
- “Push for variety, not safety. I will pick what to keep.”  
      
    

- Research Mode  
      
    

- Use when you want analysis, comparison, or structured summaries from material you provide  
      
    
- Good for:  
      
    

- Comparing two offers or plans  
      
    
- Extracting patterns from call transcripts or survey responses  
      
    
- Summarizing training materials into checklists or rubrics  
      
    

- How to ask for it:  
      
    

- “Do not invent new strategies. Only summarize, compare, and highlight patterns in what I provide.”  
      
    

For anything that smells like law, medicine, finance, regulation, or policy, assume:

You are in Standard / Evidence Mode with very low creativity.

That means:

- You bring your internal training and policies as Grounding  
      
    
- You reference external frameworks or guidelines (professional codes, regulatory docs, style guides) where appropriate  
      
    
- You say things like:  
      
    

- “Only summarize and organize what is in these documents.”  
      
    
- “Do not give medical or legal advice. Do not add new rules.”  
      
    

In more playful work (hooks, campaign ideas, copy variations), you can start in Creative Mode, then drop back to Standard / Evidence Mode for the final draft.

Now let’s plug all of that into DELTA.

---

### Step 1: D – Draft

Get The First Version On The Table

You start DELTA after you already have:

- An ALIGNED brief for a Pro task  
      
    
- A Pro prompt built from that ALIGNED brief  
      
    

Now you generate your first draft.

That is the D.

Make one MODE choice upfront:

- If this is structural, serious, or evidence based, use Standard / Evidence Mode from the start:  
      
    

- “In Standard / Evidence Mode, generate the first draft following the ALIGNED brief. Only use the Grounding sources provided.”  
      
    

- If this is creative exploration (ideas, angles), you might start in Creative Mode:  
      
    

- “In Creative Mode, generate 10 different options that follow this ALIGNED brief and our norms.”  
      
    

Either way:

- Do not chase perfection on Draft  
      
    
- Do not start editing as you read  
      
    
- Just confirm:  
      
    

- Does it follow the Deliverable structure?  
      
    
- Are the main sections there?  
      
    

Then move on. Draft is allowed to be rough.

---

### Step 2: E – Evaluate

Compare It To ALIGNED, Not Your Vibes

Most people skim a long output and go with their gut:

- “Seems okay.”  
      
    
- “Feels off.”  
      
    

Vibes are not a system.

Evaluate is where you replace vibes with structure.

You look at the draft through the ALIGNED lens:

- Audience – is it clearly addressed to the right segment and stage?  
      
    
- Lens – does it sound like the role you chose (copywriter, consultant, hiring manager), or like generic blog filler?  
      
    
- Intent – did it hit all sections, length constraints, and MODE expectations?  
      
    
- Grounding and Tools – does it use the real testimonials, metrics, policies, steps, and training? Or did it improvise?  
      
    
- Norms and Safety – any risky promises, compliance issues, off brand tone?  
      
    
- Examples and Edges – is it copying good patterns, and avoiding the “do not do this” list?  
      
    
- Deliverable – is the format exactly what you asked for?  
      
    

You can do this manually, and you can also use Research Mode to have the model help you evaluate.

For example:

“In Research Mode, evaluate this draft against the ALIGNED brief above. For each ALIGNED element (Audience, Lens, Intent, Grounding, Norms, Examples/Edges, Deliverable), list where this draft matches and where it falls short. Be strict.”

#### Using Internal And External Training In Evaluate

Here is where training comes in.

You can bring in:

- Internal training:  
      
    

- Your internal SOPs  
      
    
- Internal slide decks or manuals  
      
    
- Recorded call summaries or QA rubrics  
      
    

- External training / methodologies:  
      
    

- Omnisend or CoSchedule guidance on high performing subject lines  
      
    
- StoryBrand for messaging  
      
    
- HubSpot style guides for emails  
      
    
- Industry codes of conduct or professional standards  
      
    

You simply ground and evaluate against them.

Examples:

- For email subject lines:  
      
    

- Paste a short rubric or bullet list from an Omnisend or CoSchedule training  
      
    
- Then say:  
      
      
    “In Research Mode, evaluate these 5 subject lines against the rubric below. Score each from 0 to 100 and explain why.”  
      
      
    

- For legal or policy language:  
      
    

- Paste in your policy doc or an excerpt from an accepted template  
      
    
- Then:  
      
      
    “Evaluate this draft against the policy text below. List every place where the draft adds, changes, or omits a material rule.”  
      
      
    

You are not “training the model from scratch.”

You are saying:

“Here is the playbook. Judge this draft against it.”

---

### Step 3: L – Locate

Find The Real Problems

Evaluate gives you raw material.

Locate turns that into a focused fix list.

You are answering:

“What exactly must change before I am willing to use this?”

Three categories:

- Gaps  
      
    

- Missing sections or steps  
      
    
- Missing proof or FAQs  
      
    
- Missing required elements from your training or frameworks  
      
    

- Risks  
      
    

- Implied guarantees that are not in your policies  
      
    
- Medical, legal, or financial advice you cannot give  
      
    
- Anything that conflicts with internal or external rules  
      
    

- Weak Spots  
      
    

- Vague, generic, or “AI voice” sections  
      
    
- Confusing instructions  
      
    
- Hooks that feel flat or irrelevant  
      
    

Again, you can do this by eye, or ask in Research Mode:

“From your evaluation and the training materials provided, list the top 5 to 10 concrete issues (gaps, risks, weak spots) that must be fixed to make this draft ready to use. Flag any areas that do not match the training or frameworks.”

For example, for a newsletter subject line set:

- “Subject lines do not follow the training rules about clarity and benefit”  
      
    
- “No curiosity elements used, despite rubric”  
      
    
- “Two subject lines over the recommended length”  
      
    

For a policy doc:

- “Introduces the idea of partial refunds, which is not in policy”  
      
    
- “Fails to mention required disclaimer in section 3”  
      
    

Now you have a surgical to do list.

---

### Step 4: T – Tighten

Fix What Matters, On Purpose

Tighten is where MODE and training really pay off.

You are not saying “make it better.”

You are saying:

“Fix this list of issues, in this way, under this mode.”

Two flavors again:

1. Section by section tightening  
      
    
2. Whole draft tightening under clear constraints  
      
    

For highly regulated or sensitive content, you are in Standard / Evidence Mode with training front and center.

Example for a policy doc:

“In Standard / Evidence Mode, update this draft using the ALIGNED brief and the policy text below. Fix these issues:

- Remove all mentions of partial refunds. Our policy is ‘no refunds after 14 days’ as written.  
      
    
- Add the required disclaimer text at the end of section 3, exactly as written in the policy.  
      
    
- Remove or rewrite any advice that sounds like legal or financial advice. Replace with ‘this is general information, not legal or financial advice.’  
      
    
- Ensure every rule stated in the draft can be matched to a specific line in the policy text.  
    Do not add new policies. If something is not covered, mark it as ‘refer to policy owner’ instead of inventing a rule.”  
      
    

For subject lines using external training:

“In Creative Mode, generate 10 new subject lines for this email based on the training rubric below. Then switch to Research Mode and score each one from 0 to 100 according to the rubric. Only return the 5 that score 90 or higher, with their scores and one sentence explaining why.”

For a sales page:

“In Standard Mode, rewrite the draft using the ALIGNED brief and the list of issues below. Keep the existing structure and prices.

- Make ‘Who This Is For’ match the Audience description exactly.  
      
    
- Use at least 3 of the 4 testimonials from Grounding.  
      
    
- Remove all guarantee language and replace with our actual policy wording.  
      
    
- Add one FAQ about ‘I do not have time’ using our internal training notes.  
    Keep tone and norms from ALIGNED. Do not invent new offers or bonuses.”  
      
    

You are steering like a project lead, not begging a magic box.

---

### Step 5: A – Audit

Final Check Before You Trust It

Audit is your final gate.

At this point you have:

- An improved draft  
      
    
- A clear list of issues you supposedly fixed  
      
    
- Training and ALIGNED both in play  
      
    

Now you:

- Run a Pro level QuickCheck:  
      
    

- Relevance  
      
    
- Accuracy versus Grounding and training  
      
    
- Clarity  
      
    
- Format  
      
    

- Ask for a risk and assumption report in Research / Evidence Mode:  
      
    

“In Research / Evidence Mode, list any remaining risks, unclear claims, or assumptions that are not directly supported by the Grounding, policies, or training materials. Mark anything that might create legal, financial, or compliance issues.”

If what remains is:

- Minor wording preferences  
      
    
- Small tone tweaks  
      
    

You can fix those manually.

If something bigger pops up, you can do one more small Tighten + Audit loop.

Then you stop.

Remember:

DELTA is not infinite revision mode.  
One or two cycles. Then human editorial judgment.

Once it passes Audit, you:

- Save the final draft as an approved Pro asset  
      
    
- Save or update the Pro prompt as "ALIGNED + DELTA v1" (or v2 later)  
      
    

This is now a reusable, tested part of your LLM OS, not a one time chat.

## Pro Example 1 – Sales Page (Marketing)

Scenario:

A small consulting company offers an implementation program that is their main revenue driver. Right now, the “sales page” is a messy mix of Google Doc, old landing page, and whatever they said on the last webinar.

They want one clear, Pro level sales page they can:

- Send traffic to  
      
    
- Use as the base for emails and ads  
      
    
- Keep updated as they refine the offer  
      
    

This is perfect Pro territory.

### 1. ALIGNED Snapshot: Sales Page Brief (M-P01)

Here is the kind of ALIGNED skeleton you would save as:

Name: M-P01 Core Sales Page (Pro, ALIGNED → DELTA)

Audience  
Owners of small service businesses (consultants, agencies, local pros) doing at least some revenue, but with inconsistent sales and no clear system. They are busy, smart, and slightly embarrassed by how much of their marketing is improvised.

Lens  
You are a direct response copywriter who specializes in services for small business owners who do not want hype. You understand how to balance clarity, emotion, and proof.

Intent (+ MODE)  
In Standard Mode, create a long form sales page draft for our core implementation program. Include these sections, in order:

1. Hero (headline, subhead, CTA)  
      
    
2. The Problem / Current Chaos  
      
    
3. Why This Matters Now  
      
    
4. Our Approach / How It Works  
      
    
5. Proof (testimonials and case snippets)  
      
    
6. What You Get (modules, calls, deliverables)  
      
    
7. Who This Is For / Not For  
      
    
8. FAQs  
      
    
9. Pricing and How It Works  
      
    
10. Final CTA  
      
    

Yardsticks:

- Page should be skimmable  
      
    
- Headline options: 3 variants  
      
    
- Max 1 major CTA per main section  
      
    

Grounding and Tools  
Use:

- Program description bullets  
      
    
- 4 client testimonials  
      
    
- 2 short case study summaries  
      
    
- Current price range and payment options  
      
    
- A short bullets list of common objections from sales calls  
      
    

Do not invent guarantees, bonuses, or payment plans.

Norms and Safety  
Tone: clear, confident, human. No “10x overnight” language. No income promises. Avoid jargon. Write at an eighth grade reading level.

Examples and Edges  
Take structure inspiration from a sales page we like (short summary provided), and clarity from one of our best performing emails. Avoid vague phrases like “unlock your full potential” and “next level.” Do not talk about features we do not have.

Deliverable  
Provide a full draft with headings and subheadings. Label the 3 headline options clearly. Make CTA lines easy to spot.

That is the brief. Clear enough that if you handed it to a human copywriter, they would know what to do.

### 2. Draft and Evaluate: What Comes Back First

They run the Pro prompt with that ALIGNED skeleton.

The first draft comes back and looks… decent.

On Evaluate, using ALIGNED as a checklist, a few things show up:

- Audience: mostly right, but a few lines shift into “any business” language  
      
    
- Grounding: only 2 of 4 testimonials used, case studies barely mentioned  
      
    
- Norms: one section hints that “you will double your revenue,” which they cannot promise  
      
    
- Structure: all main sections are present, but FAQs are thin  
      
    

They also bring in an external training they trust:

- A simple framework for high converting sales page sections (from a known copywriting article)  
      
    

In Research Mode, they ask the model to evaluate:

“Compare this draft to the ALIGNED brief and this external framework summary. Where is the draft strong, where is it weak, and what is missing?”

The evaluation calls out:

- Strong: structure, overall clarity  
      
    
- Weak: hero section not specific enough, proof underused  
      
    
- Missing: clear “Who this is not for” and a strong “Why now” section  
      
    

### 3. Locate and Tighten: Give Specific Fixes

They ask the model to list the top issues:

- Hero is too generic  
      
    
- Only 2 testimonials and no case studies used  
      
    
- “Double your revenue” line conflicts with norms and policies  
      
    
- “Who this is not for” is missing  
      
    
- FAQs do not include top objections from Grounding  
      
    

Now comes Tighten.

They respond with:

“In Standard Mode, rewrite the sales page draft using the ALIGNED brief and fix these issues:

- Rewrite the hero to be specific to small service business owners with inconsistent sales.  
      
    
- Use at least 3 of the 4 testimonials and at least 1 case study snippet in the Proof section.  
      
    
- Remove ‘double your revenue’ and any similar claims. Replace with grounded language: ‘more consistent, predictable sales process.’  
      
    
- Add a ‘Who This Is Not For’ subsection with 3 bullets based on the Audience description.  
      
    
- Expand FAQs to include: ‘I do not have much time,’ ‘I tried other programs,’ and ‘What if this does not work for me?’ using the objections from Grounding.  
      
    

Keep the structure and pricing as they are. Do not add bonuses or guarantees.”

The model rewrites.

### 4. Audit and Save: Make It Reusable

On Audit, they run a final check:

- Proof: all testimonials and case studies are real and correctly used  
      
    
- Policies: no guarantees, no invented features  
      
    
- Tone: matches norms, no hype  
      
    
- Structure: all sections present and labeled  
      
    

They decide this is “good enough to ship, and we can tweak copy later.”

Then they save it in the Library as:

- Name: M-P01 Core Sales Page – Implementation Program (Pro, ALIGNED + DELTA v1)  
      
    
- Layer: Pro  
      
    
- Framework: ALIGNED → DELTA  
      
    
- Area: Marketing  
      
    
- Attachments:  
      
    

- ALIGNED brief  
      
    
- Final draft  
      
    
- Notes: “Based on external framework XYZ; last updated [date].”  
      
    

Next time they launch a new offer, they do not start from scratch. They copy M-P01, tweak Audience, Grounding, and Norms, and run DELTA again.

## Pro Example 2 – Core Client Onboarding SOP (Operations)

Scenario:

A design studio has grown to the point where multiple people handle client onboarding.

Everyone “sort of knows” the steps:

- Contract  
      
    
- Invoice  
      
    
- Intake form  
      
    
- Kickoff call  
      
    

In reality:

- Sometimes payments are missed  
      
    
- Sometimes kickoff happens before all details are collected  
      
    
- Sometimes files and access are not ready on time  
      
    

They decide to create a Pro level “Client Onboarding SOP” that new hires can follow on day one.

### 1. ALIGNED Snapshot: Onboarding SOP Brief (O-P01)

Saved as:

Name: O-P01 Client Onboarding SOP (Pro, ALIGNED → DELTA)

Audience  
Internal team: account managers and operations assistant. Some are new. They need something they can follow without guessing.

Lens  
You are an operations consultant documenting clear, step by step SOPs for small creative agencies.

Intent (+ MODE)  
In Standard / Evidence Mode, create a client onboarding SOP that a new hire can follow on their first week. Include:

- Purpose and Scope  
      
    
- Roles involved  
      
    
- Tools used  
      
    
- Step by step process from “lead says yes” to “project live in our system”  
      
    
- What to do if payment fails or forms are incomplete  
      
    
- Final checklist for “Onboarding complete”  
      
    

Grounding and Tools  
Use:

- Bullet list of current steps, even if messy  
      
    
- Tools: CRM, proposal tool, invoicing system, project management board, communication channels  
      
    
- Current payment and refund policies  
      
    
- Notes from an internal training session about “what usually goes wrong in onboarding”  
      
    

Do not invent tools or automations we do not have yet.

Norms and Safety  
Plain language. Short steps. No jargon. SOP must reflect reality, not wishful thinking.

Examples and Edges  
We include one example SOP that everyone likes from another part of the business for style reference. Hard “do not” list: do not assume automation if it does not exist, do not skip payment confirmation, do not schedule work before contract is signed.

Deliverable  
SOP with sections: Purpose, Scope, Roles, Tools, Steps, Exceptions/Edge Cases, Final Checklist.

### 2. Draft and DELTA Highlights

They run Draft.

The first SOP is a big improvement, but on Evaluate they find:

- It correctly lists tools and roles  
      
    
- It goes from “client accepted proposal” to “project loaded in project manager”  
      
    
- It never clearly states “payment confirmed” as a separate step  
      
    
- It assumes an automatic welcome email that does not exist  
      
    
- It does not say what to do if the intake form is incomplete  
      
    

They Locate issues:

- Missing explicit payment confirmation step  
      
    
- Invented automation (auto email)  
      
    
- No branch for incomplete forms  
      
    
- Checklist does not include “access confirmed” for shared drives and tools  
      
    

Tighten looks like:

“In Standard / Evidence Mode, update the SOP to:

- Add a step where the account manager or ops assistant confirms payment is received in [invoicing system] before any project setup.  
      
    
- Remove references to an automatic welcome email. Replace with a manual step where the account manager sends our existing welcome email template.  
      
    
- Add a small branch for ‘intake form incomplete’ that explains who follows up, how, and when.  
      
    
- Update the final checklist to include: contract signed, invoice paid, intake form complete, project created in [PM tool], shared folder created, access tested.  
      
    

Keep the Purpose, Scope, and Roles sections as they are.”

On Audit, they cross check against real tools and policy one more time.

Then they save it as:

- Name: O-P01 Client Onboarding SOP – Studio (Pro, ALIGNED + DELTA v1)  
      
    
- Area: Operations  
      
    
- Layer: Pro  
      
    
- Framework: ALIGNED → DELTA  
      
    

They also add a Library note: “If tools change, update Grounding + Tools and re-run DELTA.”

Now onboarding is not “tribal knowledge.” It is a Pro asset.

## Pro Example 3 – Hiring Scorecard and Interview Guide (People)

Scenario:

A growing business is hiring a customer success lead.

Until now, hiring has looked like:

- A job post they copy tweak from the last role  
      
    
- A few ad hoc interview questions  
      
    
- Gut feeling decisions  
      
    

They want:

- A clear picture of what “great” looks like in this role  
      
    
- A consistent way to interview and compare candidates  
      
    
- A scorecard they can reuse when they hire again  
      
    

### 1. ALIGNED Snapshot: Hiring Scorecard Brief (H-P01)

Saved as:

Name: H-P01 Hiring Scorecard – Customer Success Lead (Pro, ALIGNED → DELTA)

Audience  
Hiring manager and interviewers participating in the process. Some may not have hired for this role before.

Lens  
You are an experienced hiring manager for customer facing roles in small businesses.

Intent (+ MODE)  
In Standard / Research Mode, create:

1. A role scorecard with:  
      
    

- 3–5 key outcomes for the first 6–12 months  
      
    
- 5–8 competencies (skills and behaviors)  
      
    
- A simple 1–5 rating scale definition  
      
    

3. An interview question bank grouped by competency.  
      
    

Grounding and Tools  
Use:

- Role responsibilities  
      
    
- Company values  
      
    
- Notes on what has and has not worked in previous hires  
      
    
- High level expectations around metrics (retention, response times, NPS or similar)  
      
    

We also bring in external guidance from a simple hiring framework that emphasizes outcomes and competencies instead of “vibes.”

Norms and Safety  
Inclusive language. No illegal or discriminatory questions. Focus on behaviors and results, not personal life details.

Examples and Edges  
Example of a past successful hire: proactive communicator, owned problems, improved renewal rates. Example of a poor hire: waited to be told, avoided difficult conversations, dropped details.

“Do not” list: no questions about age, family, health, or personal life. Do not use vague “culture fit” without linking to clear behaviors.

Deliverable  
A scorecard as a table (Outcome/Competency, Description, 1, 3, 5 examples), plus an interview question list grouped by competency.

### 2. Draft and DELTA Highlights

Draft looks solid on first pass.

On Evaluate, they notice:

- Outcomes are slightly vague (“make customers happy”) instead of measurable  
      
    
- Some competencies overlap (“good communicator” and “strong communicator”)  
      
    
- A few questions drift into “how do you handle stress at home” territory, which they do not want  
      
    

They also run a quick pass in Research Mode:

“Using the external hiring framework summary below, evaluate these outcomes and competencies. Which ones are too vague or overlapping?”

The model flags:

- Outcomes need more specific time frames and metrics  
      
    
- Two competencies should be merged  
      
    
- 3 questions are either redundant or too personal  
      
    

Locate:

- Outcomes lack measurable definitions  
      
    
- Overlapping competencies  
      
    
- Risky or unnecessary questions  
      
    

Tighten:

“In Standard / Evidence Mode, update the scorecard and questions to:

- Rewrite each outcome to be specific and measurable over 6–12 months. Include clear signals of success.  
      
    
- Merge overlapping competencies into a single ‘Communication and Expectation Setting’ competency.  
      
    
- Remove any questions that touch on personal life outside of work. Replace with behavior based questions that focus on work situations.  
      
    

Keep the overall structure and rating scale the same.”

Audit checks:

- Are outcomes now clear enough that two interviewers could rate candidates similarly?  
      
    
- Are questions free from personal life probing and aligned with values and law?  
      
    
- Is the table structure easy to print or use on a screen during interviews?  
      
    

They save it as:

- Name: H-P01 Hiring Scorecard – CS Lead (Pro, ALIGNED + DELTA v1)  
      
    
- Area: People  
      
    
- Layer: Pro  
      
    
- Framework: ALIGNED → DELTA  
      
    

Later, when they hire for another role (for example, account manager), they copy H-P01, adjust Audience, Grounding, and a few outcomes and competencies, and run one DELTA loop.

No more starting from nothing.

## Chapter 12 – Designing Pro Prompts Faster (Pro Architect GPT)

You’ve seen what Pro can do when you hand-build it:

- ALIGNED gives you a serious brief.  
      
    
- DELTA gives you a serious refinement loop.  
      
    

The tradeoff: hand-building ALIGNED → DELTA takes energy.

This chapter is about getting that energy back.

Pro Architect GPT is your “prompt architect on call.” It:

- Interviews you for each ALIGNED element.  
      
    
- Assembles a clean Pro prompt.  
      
    
- Can even spit out DELTA helper prompts so you don’t have to remember the steps.  
      
    

You still make the decisions. It just keeps the framework on the rails when your brain is fried or your team is new.

---

### When To Reach For Pro Architect (And When Not To)

Use Pro Architect GPT when:

- You’re creating a new Pro template (sales page, core SOP, hiring scorecard, policy doc).  
      
    
- You have lots of details in your head but not much patience to structure them.  
      
    
- A team member needs to build a Pro prompt and you don’t want to teach ALIGNED from scratch.  
      
    

Don’t bother with it when:

- You’re doing small, one-off tasks → Nano + 5-Box is faster.  
      
    
- You already have a solid Pro template in your Library → just tweak Audience / Grounding and re-run.  
      
    

Think of it like this:

- Nano = you holding the hammer.  
      
    
- Pro = you plus a blueprint.  
      
    
- Pro Architect GPT = the foreman who walks you through the blueprint template so nothing important gets missed.

## The Core Flow: From Vague Idea → Pro Prompt In One Session

You’ll usually follow a simple pattern:

1. Pick a Pro-worthy task.  
      
    
2. Tell Pro Architect what you’re trying to build.  
      
    
3. Answer a short series of ALIGNED questions.  
      
    
4. Let it assemble the Pro prompt text.  
      
    
5. Have it generate DELTA helper prompts.  
      
    
6. Save everything into your Library.  
      
    

Let’s break that down.

---

### Step 1 – Pick One Pro Task (Not Ten)

First choice: what are you actually building?

Good Pro candidates:

- A flagship sales page or VSL outline.  
      
    
- A core client onboarding SOP.  
      
    
- A renewal/retention SOP.  
      
    
- A hiring scorecard + interview guide for a key role.  
      
    
- A policy doc (refund, cancellation, service levels).  
      
    

Bad candidates for Pro Architect:

- “Write three Instagram posts.” (Nano.)  
      
    
- “Help me brainstorm names.” (Nano + Creative Mode.)  
      
    

Pick one.

If you’re reading this and thinking, “My business has fires everywhere,” choose the one asset that, if improved, would remove the most headaches or move the most revenue.

That’s your Pro target.

---

### Step 2 – Start The Conversation The Right Way

Pro Architect GPT works best when you tell it what game you’re playing.

A good opening line looks like this:

“I want to create a Pro prompt using ALIGNED → DELTA for a [type of asset] in my [business type]. Ask me questions to design it. Keep your questions short and in plain English.”

For example:

“I want to create a Pro prompt using ALIGNED → DELTA for a client onboarding SOP in my bookkeeping business. Ask me questions to design it. Keep your questions short and in plain English.”

You’ve just:

- Told it this is prompt-design mode, not “do the work for me” mode.  
      
    
- Named the asset type.  
      
    
- Given it permission to drive the interview.  
      
    

From here, you mostly answer questions.

---

### Step 3 – Let It Walk You Through ALIGNED

A good Pro Architect flow feels like a focused intake form, not an interrogation.

Roughly, it should walk you through:

- Audience – Who is this for? What’s their situation? What do they care about?  
      
    
- Lens – What “hat” should the model wear? Copywriter, consultant, hiring manager, policy drafter?  
      
    
- Intent + MODE – What are we producing? How deep? Do we want Standard, Creative, or Research Mode as the default thinking style?  
      
    
- Grounding & Tools – What real inputs should it use? (Docs, metrics, policies, training, FAQs, transcripts, tools.)  
      
    
- Norms & Safety – What tone, voice, and safety rules must it follow?  
      
    
- Examples & Edges – What are good patterns to emulate and bad edges to avoid?  
      
    
- Deliverable – What format should the final output take so it’s plug-and-play for you or your team?  
      
    

Your job: answer briefly.

- 1–3 sentences per section.  
      
    
- Paste in any internal training or external frameworks you want included:  
      
    

- Internal: your slides, SOP text, policy snippets, meeting notes.  
      
    
- External: StoryBrand-style structure, an Omnisend subject-line rubric, your industry’s code of conduct, a great example page.  
      
    

You’re not “training from scratch.” You’re saying:

“Use these as the standard you follow.”

That’s enough for Pro Architect to build a strong ALIGNED skeleton.

---

### Step 4 – Have It Assemble The ALIGNED Prompt

Once you’ve answered the questions, ask:

“Now assemble everything into a single ALIGNED prompt I can reuse with any LLM. Use clear section labels and keep the language tight.”

You should get back something like:

A – Audience: …

L – Lens: …

I – Intent (+MODE): …

G – Grounding & Tools: …

N – Norms & Safety: …

E – Examples & Edges: …

D – Deliverable: …

  

[Then a ‘Final Prompt’ section that wraps it all together in one instruction block.]

This becomes:

- The Pro prompt you paste into ChatGPT, Claude, etc. when you want to generate a draft.  
      
    
- The template you paste into your Notion Library so your team can use it too.  
      
    

You can also ask it to:

“Add a short preface explaining when to use this prompt and when to use Nano instead.”

That preface makes the template more self-explanatory for team members.

---

### Step 5 – Generate DELTA Helper Prompts

You don’t want to memorize DELTA every time.

So once the ALIGNED prompt looks good, say:

“Write three short helper prompts I can use for DELTA with this asset: one for Evaluate, one for Locate, and one for Tighten. Keep them generic so I can reuse them with any draft created from this ALIGNED prompt.”

You’ll get back something like (simplified here):

- Evaluate helper  
      
      
    “Evaluate the draft above against the ALIGNED brief. For each ALIGNED element (Audience, Lens, Intent, Grounding, Norms, Examples/Edges, Deliverable), list where the draft matches and where it falls short. Be strict and specific.”  
      
      
    
- Locate helper  
      
      
    “From your evaluation, list the top 5–10 specific issues (gaps, risks, weak spots) that must be addressed to make this draft ready to use. Be concrete and actionable.”  
      
      
    
- Tighten helper  
      
      
    “Using the issues list above, rewrite the draft. Fix only those issues while preserving the overall structure and any grounded facts. Follow Norms & Safety strictly and do not invent new offers, policies, or tools.”  
      
      
    

You can tweak the wording once, then store those helpers next to the ALIGNED prompt in the Library.

Now anyone on your team can:

1. Run the Pro prompt (ALIGNED).  
      
    
2. Paste the Evaluate helper.  
      
    
3. Paste the Locate helper.  
      
    
4. Paste the Tighten helper.  
      
    

Without needing to remember DELTA by heart.

---

### Step 6 – Save It Properly In Your Library

The Pro Architect GPT session is temporary.  
Your Library is permanent.

For each finished Pro prompt, create a Notion entry with fields like:

- Name: M-P01 Core Sales Page – [Offer Name] (Pro, ALIGNED + DELTA)  
      
    
- Area: Marketing / Operations / People / Finance / Legal  
      
    
- Layer: Pro  
      
    
- Framework: ALIGNED → DELTA  
      
    
- Status: In Use / Draft / Needs Review  
      
    
- ALIGNED Prompt: full text  
      
    
- DELTA Helpers: Evaluate / Locate / Tighten snippets  
      
    
- Mode Notes: e.g. “Default: Standard Mode. For ideation, switch to Creative; for policy, switch to Standard / Evidence Mode only.”  
      
    
- Grounding Checklist: what you must collect before running it (testimonials, policies, metrics, etc.)  
      
    
- Example Output: paste one “good enough” result as a reference  
      
    

This is how you turn one Pro Architect session into a reusable, shareable asset, not just another lost chat.

## Example Walkthrough: From Messy Idea To Pro Template

Let’s walk one scenario through quickly: a renewal/retention SOP.

Starting point in your head:

“We need a consistent way to handle contracts coming up for renewal. Right now everyone does their own thing.”

You open Pro Architect GPT and type:

“I want to build an ALIGNED → DELTA Pro prompt for a client renewal/retention SOP in my service business. Ask me questions to design it. Keep questions short and in plain English.”

It asks you about:

- Who the SOP is for (account managers).  
      
    
- What systems you use (CRM, billing, email).  
      
    
- Typical renewal timelines and problems.  
      
    
- Your policies for price increases, discounts, and contract lengths.  
      
    
- What a “good renewal process” looks like to you.  
      
    

You answer in short bullets and paste in:

- Your current rough checklist.  
      
    
- An internal slide where you once explained “how we want renewals to go.”  
      
    

It then assembles an ALIGNED prompt:

- Audience: account managers  
      
    
- Lens: operations consultant  
      
    
- Intent: renewal SOP with branches  
      
    
- Grounding: your tools, policies, current process notes  
      
    
- Norms: no surprise discounts, no policy-breaking promises  
      
    
- Examples/Edges: what good and bad renewals look like  
      
    
- Deliverable: SOP with steps, branches, checklist  
      
    

You ask it for DELTA helpers.

You paste everything into your Notion Library.

Later that day, you or a team member:

1. Run the Pro prompt to get a v1 SOP.  
      
    
2. Use DELTA helpers to refine.  
      
    
3. Save the final SOP back into the same entry.  
      
    

That’s one Pro asset spun up quickly without needing to hold ALIGNED + DELTA in your working memory.

## How The Popular Frameworks Actually Compare

We’ll keep this high-level and opinionated. You don’t need to memorize any acronyms—you just need to see the pattern.

### 1. Structure vs. System

Most popular frameworks try to solve:

“How do I phrase a single prompt more clearly?”

They give you ingredients like:

- Role  
      
    
- Context  
      
    
- Objective/Intent  
      
    
- Tone/Style  
      
    
- Response/Format  
      
    
- Examples  
      
    

Those are useful—but they’re already inside Nano and Pro:

- 5-Box → WHO, GOAL + yardsticks, PROOF PACK, Voice, Format Lock.  
      
    
- ALIGNED → Audience, Lens (Role), Intent/MODE, Grounding & Tools, Norms & Safety, Examples & Edges, Deliverable.  
      
    

So:

- RICCE / CRISPE / CO-STAR / RAFT → prompt shape helpers.  
      
    
- LLM OS → business-wide workflow system (framing, grounding, QA, reuse).  
      
    

You don’t need a new framework to tell you “remember the audience” when WHO and Audience are already step one in your OS.

### 2. Data Grounding: The Big Missing Piece

None of the popular frameworks were designed specifically for your business.

They rarely make a big deal out of:

- Real client proof (testimonials, DMs, case studies).  
      
    
- Actual numbers (prices, margins, capacity, timelines).  
      
    
- Your systems (CRM, scheduler, billing, policies).  
      
    

Your LLM OS hits this hard:

- Nano → PROOF PACK is non-optional for anything important.  
      
    
- Pro → Grounding & Tools is a whole section of ALIGNED.  
      
    
- DELTA → Evaluate + Locate steps call out missing or misused proof.  
      
    

That’s why Nano/Pro beat “cool acronyms” the moment the work touches:

- Money  
      
    
- Legal-ish promises  
      
    
- Operational reality  
      
    

Without grounding, every framework is just… a nicer way to hallucinate.

### 3. Hallucination & Risk Control

Popular frameworks rarely come with:

- A built-in QA checklist.  
      
    
- A structured review loop.  
      
    
- An explicit risk lens.  
      
    

At best, they hint at “constraints” or “parameters.”

LLM OS bakes QA in at two levels:

- QuickCheck → Relevance, Accuracy-vs-Proof, Clarity, Format + footer (Used / Assumptions / Confidence).  
      
    
- DELTA → Evaluate (vs ALIGNED), Locate issues, Tighten with targeted instructions, Audit before use.  
      
    

So while other frameworks say:

“Here’s a nicer way to tell the model what you want,”

Nano + Pro say:

“Here’s how to ensure what you got is safe enough and real enough to put your name on.”

That’s a very different problem.

### 4. One Person vs. Whole Team

Most frameworks assume:

- One person.  
      
    
- One chat.  
      
    
- One moment in time.  
      
    

They don’t answer:

- “How does my VA use this?”  
      
    
- “How do my coaches or sales reps reuse this?”  
      
    
- “Where do we store the prompts that work?”  
      
    

LLM OS is intentionally team-friendly:

- Notion Library → every good Nano or Pro prompt is an entry with Area, Layer, Framework, Status.  
      
    
- Nano / Pro templates → named (M-01, S-02, O-P01, H-P01) and tagged.  
      
    
- Assistants → Nano Copilot and Pro Architect help non-nerds build good prompts.  
      
    

You’re not just “good at ChatGPT.” You’re building an internal tooling layer your team can lean on.

### 5. Mode, Reasoning, and External Training

Some frameworks dabble in “reasoning” or “examples,” but they usually treat the model as a generic brain.

LLM OS does three important extra things:

1. MODE (in ALIGNED) lets you explicitly choose:  
      
    

- Standard (just do the task well).  
      
    
- Creative (more options, more angles).  
      
    
- Research / Evidence (slower, more careful, more step-by-step).  
      
    

3. You can call for Chain-of-Thought style reasoning inside Nano/Pro when you actually want visible steps.  
      
    
4. You can anchor to external training and frameworks:  
      
    

- “Use the Omnisend email subject line guide as your standard and aim for 90%+ in their rubric.”  
      
    
- “Follow StoryBrand-style structure for the messaging.”  
      
    
- “Align this with our internal onboarding slides and SOPs.”  
      
    

So instead of learning a new prompt framework, you can:

- Reuse proven business frameworks (StoryBrand, jobs-to-be-done, sales scripts, SOP standards).  
      
    
- Plug them into Grounding, Norms, and Examples.  
      
    

That’s much more valuable than memorizing “what CRISPE stands for.”

## The Nano Prompt Quality Scorecard

Nano is your daily driver. One email. A handful of posts. A simple SOP.

The Nano Scorecard answers:

“Is this good enough to use with a light edit, or do we need to fix the prompt?”

You rate four things from 1–5:

1. Task Fit (WHO + GOAL)  
      
    
2. Grounding & Accuracy (Proof)  
      
    
3. Clarity & Voice  
      
    
4. Format & Usability  
      
    

Then you decide what to do with it.

Let’s break them down.

---

### 1. Task Fit (WHO + GOAL)

You are asking:

- “Is this clearly for the right person?”  
      
    
- “Does it actually try to achieve the goal I set?”  
      
    

Score it like this:

- 1 – Could be for anyone. Wrong audience. Wrong goal.  
      
    
- 3 – Mostly right audience, somewhat aligned to the goal, but wandering a bit.  
      
    
- 5 – Clearly for the right segment, directly aimed at the specific goal.  
      
    

Example:  
You wanted an email to re-activate past trial clients. If the copy sounds like a generic “dear subscriber” blast, that is not a 5.

---

### 2. Grounding & Accuracy (Proof)

You are asking:

- “Did it actually use the proof I gave it?”  
      
    
- “Did it make anything up?”  
      
    

Score:

- 1 – You can see invented details, fake numbers, fake guarantees, or it ignored your proof entirely.  
      
    
- 3 – Some proof used, but you notice a couple of guesses or missing details.  
      
    
- 5 – Clear use of your proof, no obvious invention, no magic guarantees.  
      
    

If you are using the Used / Assumptions / Confidence footer from QuickCheck, this is even easier to judge.

---

### 3. Clarity & Voice

You are asking:

- “Would a distracted human understand this on one read?”  
      
    
- “Does this sound like us, not a robot?”  
      
    

Score:

- 1 – Confusing, long winded, or off brand. You would not send this.  
      
    
- 3 – Understandable, but you would definitely edit.  
      
    
- 5 – Clear, simple, and feels like your brand. You would send with minor tweaks at most.  
      
    

---

### 4. Format & Usability

You are asking:

- “Can I copy and paste this somewhere and use it?”  
      
    
- “Did it follow the format I asked for?”  
      
    

Score:

- 1 – Wrong structure. Missing subject lines. Wrong table. Hard to use.  
      
    
- 3 – Mostly right, but you have to move things around.  
      
    
- 5 – Exactly the requested format. Plug and play.  
      
    

---

### Nano Overall Score: What It Means

Once you rate all four, take a rough overall Nano score:

- 1–2:  
    Do not use.  
    Redo the 5-Box. Fix WHO, GOAL, or PROOF. Re-run.  
      
    
- 3:  
    Draft only.  
    You can use it, but only after manual editing and QuickCheck.  
      
    
- 4–5:  
    Safe to ship for low to medium stakes work after QuickCheck.  
    Save in the Library as a Nano template.  
      
    

This is already a big upgrade from:

“Looks fine, I guess?”

## The Pro Prompt Quality Scorecard

Pro is different.  
You are not scoring a cute social post. You are scoring:

- A sales page  
      
    
- A core onboarding SOP  
      
    
- A hiring scorecard  
      
    
- A policy document  
      
    

This is where bad AI outputs cost money, clients, and reputation.

For Pro, we extend the scorecard. You still care about clarity and accuracy, but you add Pro specific dimensions:

1. ALIGNED Coverage  
      
    
2. Deep Grounding & Risk Handling  
      
    
3. Reusability (Template Worthy?)  
      
    
4. DELTA Pass Quality  
      
    

---

### 1. ALIGNED Coverage

You are asking:

“Does this output actually reflect what I wrote in ALIGNED?”

You check:

- Audience, Lens, Intent, Grounding, Norms, Examples / Edges, Deliverable  
      
    

Score:

- 1 – Several ALIGNED elements are missing or completely ignored.  
      
    
- 3 – Most elements are there, but a few are weak, vague, or only half obeyed.  
      
    
- 5 – The output clearly reflects all major ALIGNED pieces.  
      
    

If you read your ALIGNED spec and then the output and think “yes, this is the thing I asked for,” that is closer to a 5.

---

### 2. Deep Grounding & Risk Handling

You are asking:

- “Is this built on real data, or mostly vibes?”  
      
    
- “Does it handle risk carefully?”  
      
    

This matters a lot for:

- Pricing  
      
    
- Guarantees  
      
    
- Policies  
      
    
- Health or safety related content  
      
    
- Hiring and HR language  
      
    

Score:

- 1 – Barely uses real data, makes broad or risky claims, squishy policies.  
      
    
- 3 – Mostly grounded, but a few assumptions or mild risks remain.  
      
    
- 5 – Strong use of real data, careful wording, clear boundaries. If something is not known, it does not bluff.  
      
    

---

### 3. Reusability (Template Worthy?)

You are asking:

“Would I want my team to reuse this as a Pro template?”

Score:

- 1 – Definitely not. It is too messy, fragile, or specific to one weird situation.  
      
    
- 3 – Could be reused, but only if a senior person babysits every time.  
      
    
- 5 – Absolutely. You want this in the Library so no one has to reinvent it.  
      
    

This is where you decide if this deserves a “P-01, P-02” style slot in your system.

---

### 4. DELTA Pass Quality

You are asking:

- “Did we actually run DELTA properly, or did we just tweak it once and stop?”  
      
    

Remember, DELTA is:

- Draft  
      
    
- Evaluate  
      
    
- Locate  
      
    
- Tighten  
      
    
- Audit  
      
    

Score:

- 1 – You did not really run DELTA. Obvious issues remain.  
      
    
- 3 – You used parts of DELTA, cleaned up several issues, a few small concerns remain.  
      
    
- 5 – You clearly used DELTA end to end, addressed the key issues, and the final draft feels robust.  
      
    

---

### Pro Overall Score: What It Means

Rough overall Pro score:

- 1–2:  
    Not safe as a Pro asset.  
    Redo ALIGNED. Add better Grounding. Run DELTA again.  
      
    
- 3:  
    Draft only.  
    Internal discussion tool, not a published standard. Needs another DELTA loop and human review.  
      
    
- 4–5:  
    Approved Pro asset.  
    This is where White Team should sign off and mark it as a standard in your Library.

## The “Which One When?” Flowchart

Now to the practical part.

You are looking at a task and asking:

“Do I use Nano or Pro? And who needs to be involved?”

Here is the flowchart in plain language.

---

### Step 1 – Ask About Stakes And Reuse

Two questions:

1. “If this goes wrong, what happens?”  
      
    
2. “Will this be reused by my team or automations?”  
      
    

If the answer is:

- Low impact (small typo, one email, one post)  
      
    
- One off use  
      
    

Then start with Nano.

If the answer is:

- Money, contracts, pricing, policies, refunds  
      
    
- Health or safety advice  
      
    
- Hiring, firing, HR  
      
    
- Something you will reuse for months  
      
    

Then this is a Pro candidate.

---

### Step 2 – Check Sensitivity

Ask:

“Does this touch any of these?”

- Money, refunds, pricing, guarantees  
      
    
- Legal-ish policies (terms, contracts, promises)  
      
    
- Health or safety guidance  
      
    
- Hiring, performance, termination, or anything about people  
      
    

If yes, that is a strong Pro + Color Team moment:

- Pro with ALIGNED → DELTA  
      
    
- Red / Black / White Team need to be in your mental loop.  
      
    

Red: How does this break or get abused?  
Black: What are the policy, ethical, or legal angles?  
White: Who approves or sets the rule here?

---

### Step 3 – Decide The Layer

Use this quick rule of thumb:

Use Nano when:

- One off or low stakes  
      
    
- Daily content: emails, social posts, basic SOP drafts  
      
    
- Internal messaging that a human will always review  
      
    

Use Pro when:

- Core sales pages, main offer descriptions, major campaigns  
      
    
- Onboarding / delivery / billing SOPs  
      
    
- Hiring scorecards, job posts, onboarding plans  
      
    
- Policies and anything a lawyer or regulator would care about  
      
    

When in doubt, ask:

“Would I be annoyed if this was wrong for three months before I noticed?”

If yes, lean toward Pro.

---

### Step 4 – Decide The Quality Loop

Once you pick the layer:

- Nano:  
    QuickCheck is mandatory for anything client facing.  
    Use the Nano Scorecard if you are training a VA or want to improve.  
      
    
- Pro:  
    At least one full DELTA loop is mandatory.  
    Use the Pro Scorecard to decide if it is ready to be a template or needs another pass.  
      
    

---

### Step 5 – Overlay the Color Teams

Now add the mental Team hats on top of this:

- Red Team  
    “How does this fail, get abused, confuse people, or harm trust?”  
    Used on all Pro assets, plus important Nano pieces like big promos.  
      
    
- Blue Team  
    “How do we improve the prompt and flow?”  
    Lives inside Nano + Pro. This is where you refine 5-Box and ALIGNED.  
      
    
- Black Team  
    “What about policies, ethics, compliance, hiring fairness, data use?”  
    Used whenever you touch money, people, safety, or rules.  
      
    
- White Team  
    “What is our standard? What is officially approved?”  
    Used to mark Score 4–5 Pro assets as “Approved in Library.”  
      
    
- Green Team  
    “Where else can this be used now that it works?”  
    Used after you have an asset scoring 4–5 consistently.  
      
    

So the flow often looks like:

- Owner or lead chooses Nano vs Pro using the flowchart  
      
    
- Blue Team mode improves the prompt and process  
      
    
- Red / Black Team mode attacks and tests it  
      
    
- White Team mode approves it  
      
    
- Green Team mode spreads it  
      
    

You do not need separate people for each Team. These are thinking modes.  
Sometimes you wear all five hats in one afternoon.

- If 3: keep as draft, improve with Blue Team thinking  
      
    
- If 4–5: mark in your Library as “Nano – Approved”  
      
    

4. For the Pro prompt:  
      
    

- Run the Pro Scorecard  
      
    
- If ≤ 2: redo ALIGNED and Grounding, rerun DELTA  
      
    
- If 3: internal draft only, go through at least one more DELTA pass  
      
    
- If 4–5: mark as “Pro – Approved” and note White Team approval  
      
    

6. In your Notion Library, add three fields:  
      
    

- Layer: Nano / Pro  
      
    
- Quality Score: 1–5  
      
    
- White Team Approved: Yes / No (for Pro)  
      
    

That is enough to start making your LLM OS measurable.

Chapter 17 – Organizing by Area: Marketing, Sales, Ops, People, Planning

Your Prompt Library is built.  
Now we need to stop it from becoming “that Notion thing nobody opens.”

The trick is simple.  
Organize it the same way your business runs:

- Marketing  
      
    
- Sales  
      
    
- Operations / Delivery  
      
    
- People (Hiring, HR-ish)  
      
    
- Planning / Strategy  
      
    

If your Library feels like a tool drawer, your team will use it.  
If it feels like a junk drawer, they will go back to random ChatGPT chats.

This chapter is how you get the tool drawer.

---

## Why Areas Matter More Than “Prompt Type”

Most people default to organizing prompts by:

- Model (ChatGPT vs Claude)  
      
    
- Framework (RICCE vs 5-Box vs ALIGNED)  
      
    
- Format (email, post, SOP, etc.)  
      
    

That all matters, but your team does not think like that.

They think:

- “I need to send a follow up to leads.” (Sales)  
      
    
- “I need a job post for a new coach.” (People)  
      
    
- “I need a reactivation email.” (Marketing)  
      
    
- “I need to document how we onboard clients.” (Ops)  
      
    

So the Library should be organized by Area first.  
Framework and Layer (Nano vs Pro) are there, but they live in the background.

Our goal for each Area:

- 2 to 4 Nano prompts used weekly  
      
    
- 1 to 2 Pro prompts used whenever you create or update core assets  
      
    

That alone gives you a powerful, focused Library.

In Chapter 16 you created your Prompt Library database, with an Area field.

- Planning  
      
    

Everything in the Library gets one of these.  
No “General” category unless you truly have no idea where it goes. And even then, try to choose something.

Then you build Notion views that filter by Area, so:

- The marketing person lives in the Marketing view

## Common Pitfalls To Avoid

A few easy ways to mess this up, and how to fix them.

Pitfall 1: Everything goes in “General.”  
Fix: Force yourself to pick an Area. Even if it is imperfect. You can always change it later.

Pitfall 2: Over building in one Area and ignoring others.  
Fix: Get two Areas functional first. Then rotate attention. For example:  
Quarter 1: Marketing + Sales.  
Quarter 2: Ops + People.

Pitfall 3: Not telling people where to live.  
Fix: For every role, define one view as “home base.”  
Example:
